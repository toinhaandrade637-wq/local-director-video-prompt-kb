ABRIGO — The Coat.
A mother knits her  son a crimson coat, one size too large. It stays one size too large through every age of his life — the same coat, always a little too big, always worn. A single crimson thread runs from her knitting needles to the coat itself, visible in every frame. Not a mystery. Not a twist. The premise: love, as a physical connection with a physical length. No spoken words in the entire film — everything lives in sounds and gestures. No music, anywhere. One thread, from the very first frame.




Why this film is hard — and why we chose it anyway




We picked something that is difficult by nature. Recreating original animation with AI is a fundamentally different problem from generating live action. Reality is singular: there is reality, and there are visual effects — ask a model for a realistic cinematic shot and it knows exactly what you mean. Animation is the opposite. There are as many art styles as there are textures, frame cadences, hybrids and visual languages; the space is effectively infinite. Asking AI for "digital art" is asking for nothing.




You can see it across this festival: in every competitor who chose animation, the fight to hold a style is visible on screen — and we admire every single one of them for taking this road. It is a hard road, and everyone walking it knows it.




Achieving a style was complex. The principle behind ours is simple to state and demanding to execute: the closer a character sits to the target visual style, the more the model holds that style in motion — characters animating on twos with stepped timing, a 2.5D feel, 2D particles and hand-drawn effects living inside a painted 3D environment. So we encoded the style as a DNA: a canonical block of technique declarations that opens every prompt in the project, adapting per shot without ever losing its core. The DNA is not a description of a look. It is a set of laws the image must obey.




Sometimes the caprice of making the thing you truly want comes at a price. But seeing the result is something that fills you — every time.




The film







We follow the son through three ages — toddler, child, adult — as the coat protects him from everything. The crimson thread never touches him; it runs from the mother's needles to the coat itself — and since he wears the coat everywhere, always, the distinction never mattered. Until it does. As an adult, on the night his coworkers invite him out, he discovers the thing the film has been hiding in plain sight: the coat comes off. It always could. He takes it off, leaves it hanging at the office, and walks out into his first night unwatched — while at the other end of the thread, for the first time in her life, the mother feels nothing at all. He carried the coat all those years because he never confronted the act of taking it off. And when the coat finally passes to her, it fits her perfectly. It was always her size.




The message: overprotection is love at the wrong scale. It doesn't get cut or destroyed — it gets carried at the right size. And it survives on the child's side of the thread too.




Because the film uses mimic language at all — laughter, gasps, a sob and gestures carry every scene — a festival audience anywhere reads a thread, a coat and a mother's hands identically. Nothing needs translation.
