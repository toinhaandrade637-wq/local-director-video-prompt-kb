REALISM WAS THE ONLY BRIEF

I did not set out to make an AI film. I set out to find whether a film made this way could

stop announcing itself.

Everything was tested against one bar — performance, world, and story. Not whether a shot

looked impressive. Whether it looked photographed. Whether a face carried something before

it said anything. Whether the world held together when nobody was explaining it.




If the thought "this is AI" crosses your mind at any point — even faintly, even underneath

everything else — that shot failed, and I would rather know.




That bar is why the light in this film is unflattering, why the camera is never steady, why

nothing is symmetrical, and why the failure log below is longer than the credits.




If you noticed the tool, I failed.

IMAGINATION IS THE ONLY BUDGET LEFT

Every film I have ever wanted to make began with someone telling me what we could not

afford.

A forty-metre mountain of discarded machines under a flyover, in the rain, at night. Five

machines rising out of a flooded tunnel. A hall holding forty thousand vessels. Twenty

years ago each of those was a conversation that ended in no. This time nobody said no,

because there was nobody left to ask.

Locations, props, costumes, lighting, casting, sound — things a production spends months

and crores on — were available to me in an afternoon, alone, at a desk in Bengaluru.

I am still not over it.

And it cuts both ways. When nothing is impossible, the only thing left to blame is your

own taste.

✨ Context

By 2047 nobody in Bengaluru gives birth. Children are grown in facilities and sold. It is cheaper, it is safer, and nobody argues with it anymore.

This project is an attempt to build a whole world properly before shooting a frame of it, and to do it alone.

Chapter 01:  The Beginining




It began the way these things always begin — with people who could not have children, and

a way to help them.

Then it was a way to buy time. Freeze your eggs at thirty, decide at forty. Nobody argued.




Then it was a way to remove the risk. Then the pain. Then the nine months themselves —

because if a machine could carry a child more safely than a body could, what exactly was

the body for?




The company that solved it called itself NINE. The name is the duration it abolished.




It was never an Indian company. It was an international one that understood arithmetic: a

billion and a half people, a culture built around children, and a generation of women who

had been told their whole lives what their bodies were for.




The doubt lasted about two years. Then a film star stood outside a clinic and said she was

pregnant — just not in here — and laughed, and the country laughed with her.







Chapter 01 — The ones that never pretended

The first machines that lived with us did not have faces, and nobody wanted them to.

A supply cabinet on castors that carried medicine down a hospital corridor. A therapeutic companion built as a baby seal, because in trials people responded to it more warmly than they did to anything shaped like a person.

They were equipment. They were named, and repaired, and cried over when they were finally scrapped. Nobody planned for that part.

Chapter 02 — Then we gave them faces

Faces were not added for the machines. They were added for us.

Instruction is easier to accept from something that looks back at you. Care is easier to receive. The first human-faced units went into elderly care, and one of them was deliberately built to look seventy years old, so the people it was sold to would not feel condescended to.

It worked. That was the problem.

Chapter 03 — The womb was a medical problem first

Artificial gestation was solved for good reasons. It began with women who could not carry safely, and it saved them.

Then it was cheaper. Then it was safer. Then it was screened, scheduled and guaranteed. Then it was the sensible thing to do, and then it was the thing everyone did, and the generation after that could not name a single woman who had given birth.

Nobody banned it. Nobody had to.

Chapter 05 — And then they got old

Machines built to look like people wore out on schedule, the way machines do.

There was no law protecting them, because you cannot be cruel to property. They were returned, processed and replaced. Some of them queued for it themselves, holding a paper docket, in the rain, with nobody guarding them.

The ones that were not collected went to the heap. Forty metres of them, under a flyover, in the water. The oldest ones look like tools. The newest ones look like us. They are all in the same heap.

🎭 Narrative

Roopa refuses to buy her son.

She gives birth to him illegally in a flooded tunnel two hours before the film begins, and by the time we meet her she is already running, dragging him through black water while the city's machines hunt her. She hides him at the foot of a forty-metre mountain of discarded androids and walks away.

The machines find him first.

The film is about disposability. About children turned into product, and about the people-shaped things we build to be loved and then leave out in the rain. The oldest machines look like tools. The newest ones look like us. They are all in the same heap.

🎨 Visuals

District 9, not Blade Runner.

The palette is locked to yellow, grey and brown. Rust, oxide, wet concrete, dirty amber sodium. Heavily desaturated, bleach-bypass, crushed blacks, deliberately harsh and unflattering light. Handheld documentary realism. No teal-orange anywhere in the film.

There are three sanctioned exceptions and only three. A green pulse on the child's monitor, which in most frames is the only colour on screen. The sickly green-white of the corporation's advertising. And the hot saturated gold of the one warm, dry room in the film.

The rule that governs everything: everything in this world is drowned, rusted and worn, except what belongs to the corporation, which rain cannot stick to. Every Nine vehicle beads water and stays dry while the world around it is soaked. That single physical behaviour carries the entire class argument without a line of dialogue.

Four generations of machine, grounded in real robotics history rather than invented from scratch.

Generation one was equipment that never pretended. A hospital supply cabinet on castors. A therapeutic companion built as a baby seal, because people responded to it better than they did to a person.

Generation two was labour. Squat four-legged sanitation chassis, brush arms, blank armoured head pods, no faces.

Generation three was the first built to look like people. An elderly companion unit finished as a woman in her seventies, made deliberately old so the elderly would not feel condescended to.

Generation four was finished to pass. Poreless skin, perfect bilateral symmetry, a hairline seam at each wrist, and a small engraved stock code on the inside of the arm.

Damage vocabulary: every break on a machine reveals machinery, never injury. Dry filament, corroded alloy, grey dust. No fluid, no gloss, nothing red. Rain runs down a face and stops dead at the split in the throat, and the cavity inside stays dry.

Continuity method: every location exists as a matched front and reverse plate before any shot uses it. A maximum of four references resolving in any single frame. Wardrobe held as its own element separate from the character, because the character sheet alone would not hold it.

🎬 Production

Schedule. Production began mid-August 2026. Screenplay locked 17 August. Element library, all location plates and the full teaser generated across the following two weeks. Delivery 3 September 2026, roughly three weeks end to end.

Locations. Nine environments, every one built as a matched front and reverse plate: the scrap mountain, forty metres of discarded machines under a flyover stack; the flooded commercial canyon; the dead road tunnel, the waterline and the bottomless pit beneath it; the Vidhana Soudha forecourt; the gestation facility, burning; a casino nightclub and a private members' lounge; a gestation showroom, where children are bought; a decommissioning yard, where machines queue to be scrapped; and a holding cell.

Crew. One person. Written, directed, produced, art-directed, cast, shot and edited by Narasimha Murthy G. No unit, no camera, no location shoot, no cast.

Logistics. Built entirely on Higgsfield. Seedance 2.0 for motion, GPT Image 2 and Seedream for plates and reference sheets, ElevenLabs for voice. Assembly, sound and grade conventional. Output 21:9, 4K.

📝 Notes — the failure log

This is the part worth stealing. Almost every failed shot on this film traced back to our own prompt language, not to the model. The log, in case it saves anyone else the credits.

Stabilising phrases kill motion. "Held steady in frame" made a thrown crowbar stop rotating entirely. Fixed by counting the rotations out loud in the prompt and adding that it becomes visibly smaller as it travels.

Negation backfires. Describe what you want, never what you don't. And if your interface has no separate negative field, a negative prompt block is read as things you want.

Describing a progression invites change. Saying clothing was drying told the model that wardrobe was a variable, and it started changing. State the costume as fixed, in every cut.

Absent elements mean reinvented geography. A pit sequence failed four times because the edge of the pit did not exist as an element. The model invented a crossable gap every single generation.

The image always wins over the text. When an element's written description and its picture disagree, the picture is what renders. Fix the picture.

Thin, tower, lattice and widely spaced will build you a radio antenna. We asked for a bridge of interlocked arms and got a mast with rungs. Fixed by counting tiers explicitly, packing bodies shoulder to shoulder, and removing every word that suggested a structure rather than a crowd.

Spray buries a vehicle. Two enormous walls of water hid a car completely. Water has to be kept low and behind, explicitly clear of the bodywork.

Too many jobs in one cut means the cut drops one. Past four or five cuts in fifteen seconds, the model silently renders the easy beats and bins the hard ones.

Four references per frame is the ceiling, but it is per frame, not per prompt. A multi-cut prompt can carry many more, as long as each cut only calls three or four.

Flat even light on a smooth surface makes a plastic toy. The fix was a hard directional key, deep falloff, weak fill, an 85mm lens, real cloth instead of moulded garments, and rust bleeding down the skin.

Multiple faces on one sheet blend into each other. Arms and objects tolerate sharing a frame. Faces do not.

Duplicate element names are silently random-selected. We had a lead character existing three times under one name and could not work out why his face kept changing.

A dark subject against a dark background is invisible. Obvious in theory, expensive in practice.
