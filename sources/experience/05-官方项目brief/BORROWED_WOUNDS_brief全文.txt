Every cut meant for Sadao Kiri opens on Rokuro Hane instead. That is the law of Onaga, and

Hane has knelt at the edge of the sand for twenty-six years so his swordsman can fight

without bleeding. In the final of the tournament there is no clean skin left on him, and

Kiri does the one thing the law does not forgive: he kneels for the man who has always

knelt for him.

✨ Context

In the duels of Onaga, no swordsman fights alone. Each one enters the sand with a second who kneels
at the edge, and for as long as that second keeps his knees on the ground, every wound meant for the
swordsman opens on the second instead, in the same place and to the same depth. The second may stand
up whenever he wants. The moment he does, the duel ends, his lord loses, and the loss is a disgrace
that follows him out of the field.

🎭 Narrative

Rokuro Hane is fifty. For twenty-six years he has knelt for Sadao Kiri, and nineteen victories have

left his body covered in scars, many of them opened on top of older ones. Before every duel he

spreads the same linen cloth under his knees, darkened and stiffened by decades of blood.




On the morning of Kiri's duel, Hane watches another second stand up after the first wound. The fight

stops instantly. As the defeated swordsman walks off the sand in disgrace, the people at the edge

call his second a coward, and Hane turns his face away in contempt. When the young man passes close

and looks for his eyes, Hane does not give them.




Hours later Kiri faces Toshiro Mabe, twenty years younger. Hane spreads the linen and kneels. Kiri

puts an open palm on his shoulder and says three words. The duel begins, and every cut that should

open Kiri's skin opens on Hane instead: the shoulder, the chest, and finally the neck. The neck is

the line the film has been counting toward: after it, there is no clean skin left on Hane to take

the next one. For the first time in twenty-six years Hane lifts his face toward the man he has spent

half his life protecting. Kiri reads that look as endurance, gives him a short nod, and attacks

again.




What Hane has to do now is stand up. He cannot. And his hand, slipping off his thigh, comes down on

a fold of the cloth he has knelt on for twenty-six years and never once turned over.

🎨 Visuals

The duel is in the frame and the film is not about it. Forty per cent of the running time is eight

continuous handheld takes of steel, and every wound they land opens somewhere else, on a man who is

not fighting.




LIGHT. Natural daylight only, one source, and its disc is never in frame. At dawn the sun has just

risen behind the pine trunks and rakes almost horizontally: every trunk throws a ten-metre shadow and

cuts the sand into bright bars and dark bars, warm on what it touches and cold blue on what it

misses, four stops between the two. At noon the same sun sits almost overhead, the shadows shorten to

a hard line under the brow and the nose, and a weak bounce off the pale sand fills the underside of

every face. No fill, no rim light, no lens flare. The move from one hour to the other is the only

lighting change in the film, and it works as the clock.




CAMERA. Spherical primes from 35mm to 100mm, and one height for the whole film: the chest of a

standing man. The camera stays up there while Hane goes down, so every shot of the kneeling man is

looked at from where the onlookers are standing. That height breaks exactly three times, and each

break is a beat: twice straight down over the cloth, and once at the kneeling man's own eye level,

for the only moment in the film when one person touches another.




COLOUR. A world with almost no chroma and one saturated colour that lives only in cloth: pale raked

sand, undyed linen, dark robes and a wall of near-black pine read as a single neutral scale, and the

only red in the frame is the deep madder of set-in staining. No fourth colour anywhere. Ninety parts

of the frame to ten.




CLOTH. No cut is ever seen happening: it is seen arriving. First a thin line

opening by itself on the skin, then the linen darkening from the inside by capillary action, fast at

first and slower after, and never a drop that falls. The audience learns to watch the cloth before

the face, and that habit decides who in the room understands the last thirty seconds

first.

🎬 Production

Video is generated with Seedance 2.5 at 720p, one shot per prompt, with the character and location

references attached so identity and wardrobe hold across the film. Stills are generated with Nano

Banana Pro at 4K.




The film is built on 83 reference images: character plates in three views, individual portraits,

wardrobe sheets, props, and five locations. On top of those

there are 22 wound-state plates, one per stage of damage on each body, so the same cut can be shown

open on one man and closed on another without the geometry drifting between shots.




The film was boarded before the final generation pass: 146 frames, three to five per short shot and

six to twelve per long one, in the film's own 21:9 frame, so the cut could be read shot by shot and

beat by beat. An earlier pass of 21 clips was generated, measured with ffmpeg, and thrown out: the

average shot ran 21 seconds and 191 of those seconds never cut at all. That measurement is why the

film was rebuilt from 12 shots into 38, and why the average shot is now 7.1 seconds.




Locations are two states of the same ground, dawn and noon, plus the exit path, the raked sand in

macro and the strip of trodden earth where the onlookers stand. No sets are reused from anywhere

else and no stock material is used.

📝 Notes

The title was chosen for what it names. Borrowed Wounds names the mechanism, and borrowed carries the

whole theme in one word: what is borrowed belongs to someone else and has to be given back. Three

earlier candidates were dropped for the same reason in three different forms. Each of them answered

on the poster a question the film spends four and a half minutes asking.
