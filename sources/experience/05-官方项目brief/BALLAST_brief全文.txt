A hermit crab stranded on a littered beach builds wings from washed-up scraps, longing for the freedom of the seabirds overhead. His journey leads him to a better life—one he refuses to leave his friend behind to enjoy.

✨ Context.

BALLAST is a solo-produced animated short created for the Higgsfield Global Film Festival.

The creative goal was a complete, emotionally readable film with the consistency and polish associated with professional feature animation. Every element—character design, physical action, lighting, color, environments, and sound—was developed to support one coherent experience.

The ambition was sustained quality across the film. Characters needed to remain recognizable, locations needed to feel connected, and individual shots needed to serve the story. The audience should be able to settle into Sean’s world and care about his journey.

Higgsfield awarded the project 20,000 generation credits, which supported the iteration needed to reach its final quality. 

The completed master is in 1080p.




🐦 Narrative

Logline

A hermit crab builds wings from washed-up litter, seemingly dreaming of flight. After rescuing a young seabird, he finally experiences the freedom he envies—and discovers a cleaner shore where he and his friend can begin again.

Story

Sean, a small orange hermit crab, lives on a beach littered with human waste. His shell is dirty and weathered, but his surroundings do not reflect the life he would choose.

His carefully decorated home reveals this distinction. He arranges shells and found objects with care, creating beauty from what is available. He has taste, resourcefulness, and aspirations. He is already doing everything he can to improve his circumstances.

Above him, seabirds come and go freely. Sean watches them with envy. They can leave this beach whenever they want. He cannot.

The film initially lets the audience understand his desire as a simple dream of flying. Its deeper motivation remains embedded in the environment: the littered beach, his worn shell, and the effort he puts into making his home beautiful.

Clickety, a purple hermit crab wearing a red bucket as an improvised shell, watches Sean and follows his activity with curiosity.

Sean gathers discarded materials and constructs a pair of wings. The building and flight attempt unfold in warm, idealized light. His invention briefly seems capable of turning longing into possibility.

The attempt fails. Sean crashes, and the wings are damaged. Clickety comes to sit beside him. The birds remain occupied with their own lives while the weather begins to turn.

A storm brings a more immediate danger. The young seabird is stranded on a rock and overwhelmed by the surf. Sean enters the water to help.

Underwater, he reaches the chick and brings it back to the surface and shore. His actions change his relationship with the flock: the creature who watched them from below has saved one of their own.

After the storm, the beach passes through night into a soft pink dawn.

The birds gather on their driftwood perch. The juvenile acknowledges Sean and invites him to come with them. He boards an adult bird and takes to the sky.

For a time, the film gives him the experience the audience believes he has wanted all along: flight among the seabirds, carried above the shoreline into open air.

Then something below catches his attention.

The audience does not immediately see what he has found. His joy becomes purposeful excitement. He signals that he wants to go back.

Sean returns to the littered beach and finds Clickety. He invites his friend to join him. Clickety’s initial reserve gives way to excitement, and the two board the bird together.

The flock carries them to a different stretch of coast: a cleaner, welcoming beach scattered with natural shells.

Here, the meaning of Sean’s journey becomes clear. Flight was the route he imagined toward a better life. This is the place he was hoping to reach.

Sean and Clickety each choose a new shell. They leave behind Sean’s old, dirt-crusted shell and wing rig, and Clickety’s bucket. The birds depart, and the friends wave goodbye before walking into their new surroundings.

The final image holds on what they have left behind.

They have dropped their ballast.

Narrative intention

The apparent dream of flight is a deliberate misdirection. The audience first follows a familiar ambition, then reinterprets it when Sean reaches the cleaner beach.

His decorated home is an early clue: he has always wanted a better life and has already tried to create one within his limited circumstances. His envy of the birds concerns their freedom to leave.

The ending fulfills that ambition. The wings have served their purpose, even though they never worked as he intended. His new shell makes the change tangible, and bringing Clickety ensures that this better life is shared.

Message

The desire for a better life can begin with making beauty where you are, even while longing for the freedom to leave. Reaching that life means recognizing what is worth carrying forward—and what can finally be set down.

Friendship belongs in the future Sean chooses. The weight of his old circumstances does not.

Audience

An all-ages visual fable, accessible through action, expression, and environment, with a second layer of meaning that becomes clear at the destination.

🎨 Visuals

Mood, color palette, and creative direction.

The color-phase system

The palette follows distinct emotional phases:

Phase 1 — Natural: The opening beach, Sean’s home, and his early preparations establish everyday life. The environment makes the contrast between his circumstances and his aspirations visible.
Phase 2 — Golden / Glorified: Wing construction and the flight attempt receive warmer, slightly idealized light. This phase shares Sean’s belief in the possibility he is building.
Phase 3 — Storm / Grey: The failed attempt and approaching storm remove that warmth. The underwater rescue introduces a distinct blue-green environment as the story shifts from personal ambition to urgent action.
Phase 4 — Pink Dawn / Renewal: After the storm, softer pink-gold light accompanies the invitation, flight, and journey toward a better home. The warmth now belongs to an experienced possibility rather than an imagined one.

Cinematography

The camera language draws on close study of animated filmmaking, including Up: allowing expressions time to register, using stillness at important emotional moments, and letting composition communicate relationships.

Physical distance helps track the characters’ connection. Close-ups make small changes in Sean’s and Clickety’s expressions readable. Wider frames establish the scale of the beach and the freedom represented by the birds.

Withholding information is central to the flight sequence. When Sean notices the new destination, the audience sees his reaction before seeing its cause. His decision to return for Clickety comes before the full explanation of what he has found.

Art direction

The environments carry narrative information.

Sean’s decorated home establishes his care and aspiration. The littered beach establishes what he wants to escape. The cleaner destination and available shells reveal what a better life looks like in practical terms.

The characters’ shells also tell their stories. Sean carries a worn natural shell; Clickety makes do with a discarded bucket. Their new shells make the ending’s change visible without explanation.

Sean’s orange coloring, Clickety’s purple coloring and red bucket, and the juvenile bird’s smaller scale provide immediate visual identification.

Title reveal

A cream-white wrapper with orange lettering moves through the beach environment to reveal the title. The title is introduced on the same discarded material that defines Sean’s surroundings and supplies his invention.

Character and flock continuity

The principal characters retain consistent identities, proportions, colors, and defining objects throughout the film. The flock’s established full group comprises six adults and one smaller juvenile; framing and scene geography determine which birds are visible in individual shots.

Sound

Sound design supports the physical world and the emotional progression. Environmental detail, material interactions, character movement, and changes in weather help connect the generated shots into a continuous experience.




🎬 Production

Workflow, tools, and how this was actually built.

Solo production, AI-generation tools throughout for both video and previsualization, prompts
and creative direction developed conversationally, iteratively, shot by shot.

The prompt system. Every generation follows a locked structure (scene context,
active references, location geography, camera and action broken down beat by beat, performance,
physics, lighting, color, audio, style, and a positive/negative lock pass at the end that restates
every fact genuinely at risk of drifting). This structure is documented as its own reusable skill
and was refined specifically from what reliably generated well versus what didn't.

The Blender previz experiment — attempted, partially abandoned, then selectively readopted.
Blender blocking was brought in to solve a real weakness of prose-only prompting: imprecise camera
timing on shots needing exact velocity-matching or multi-camera geographic consistency. It failed
on the film's opening sequence specifically because that scene has two characters moving through
shared space in different directions — a harder problem than initially appreciated, worsened by
this project's low-to-the-ground crab anatomy lacking the clear human landmarks (head, shoulders,
gaze) that make spatial tracking easier for other video-generation work. Rather than abandon the
tool entirely, the failure was diagnosed precisely: multi-subject relative geometry is the
specific weakness, not 3D blocking in general. Blender previz was reintroduced, narrowly and
deliberately, for later shots whose problem profile actually suits it — single-subject scenes
needing exact camera-speed matching, like the wing-reveal launch sequence and the film's emotional
high point, the full-flock flight.

Iterative craft, not first-pass generation. Several shots required multiple, carefully
diagnosed correction passes — not just "make it better" but identifying the actual mechanism of
failure and fixing that specific thing. Notable examples: a reflection effect that initially
turned a character's eyes into literal mirrors, corrected by confining the effect to the eye's
natural glossy highlight; a character who began speaking despite an absolute no-dialogue rule,
traced to open-mouth smile language and fixed by locking every expression to a closed mouth; a
"look down" beat that repeatedly resolved as looking at the wrong nearby object, eventually solved
by changing the direction of the beat entirely rather than continuing to refine the same
instruction.
