How to make an AI video from a script?

The process has five steps: format the script, split it into scenes, build a shot list, generate the shots, and assemble the final cut. The most common mistake is pasting the whole script into a text-to-video prompt box. The model reads it as one long description and returns a single generic clip, with no cuts and no story structure.

A script asks more from a tool than a single clip. It needs the same character in every scene, a consistent look across shots, and control over where the cuts land. Here is each step in order.

How do you prepare a script for AI video?

A working script has four beats: hook, problem, solution, call to action. This shape fits ads, explainers, and short stories, because it gives every scene a clear job.

Hook (first scene). One strong visual that shows what the video is about, without setup or logos.
Problem (one or two scenes). The tension the viewer recognizes. Keep it concrete: a person, a situation, an obstacle.
Solution (two or three scenes). The turn. Your product, idea, or character resolves the tension on screen.
CTA (final scene). One clear action, spoken or shown as text.

For spoken video, around 60 to 80 words per 30 seconds is a useful starting range, but read the script aloud and time it, because delivery speed and pauses change the final length.

Formatting matters as much as structure. Give every scene a header with the location and time of day, keep action lines short and visual, and hold each scene to one idea. If a scene needs two ideas, it works better as two scenes. Write what the camera can see: "she feels conflicted" leaves the model without direction, while "she stops mid-step and looks back at the door" describes a shot.

How to turn a script into a storyboard or shot list?

A shot list turns each scene into camera instructions: shot type, camera move, who is in frame, and what happens. This is the stage that decides whether the result reads as a film or as a set of unrelated clips.

A simple shot list has five columns: scene number, shot type (wide, medium, close-up), camera move (static, pan, dolly), character, and action. One scene usually becomes two to four shots. A hook can be a single wide shot, while a solution scene might need a medium shot, a close-up reaction, and an insert.

How do you generate the video from your shot list?

Generation goes scene by scene, and project-level settings keep all scenes looking like one film. In Cinema Studio 4.0 a single generation runs up to 30 seconds at up to 1080p, enough to carry a full scene, and it can come back already cut into several shots depending on the tempo you set. The order below works for most projects.

Step 1. Set the genre. Genre changes how the model handles light, framing, and pacing. Drama holds the camera closer and longer, Action ties it to the moving subject, Horror withholds the frame and lets the shadow do the work. Without a genre, the output tends to land in a flat middle ground.

Step 2. Set the era. Choose the decade the shot is set in, and grain, color, and lens style adjust to match. The same scene set in the 80s and in the 2010s comes out as two different films.

Step 3. Configure the camera. Choose a camera type (Modern, 35mm Film, 8mm Film, DV Camcorder), a lens, and an aperture, then pick from 30+ camera movement presets. These settings apply at generation time, so the prompt only needs to describe the scene itself.

Step 4. Set the tempo. Tempo controls how often the edit cuts inside a generation: Chaotic for action, Dynamic for commercial pacing, Calm for drama, Single Shot for one continuous take.

Step 5. Direct the emotions. Tag a character in the prompt with one of 8+ emotions, for example @character_name Joy or @character_name Anger, and the model builds it into the expression and body language. Without a tag, AI characters tend to default to a neutral expression.

Step 6. Generate with references. One generation accepts up to 50 reference images. Use them to lock a face, a product, or a style before the first frame renders. This is what keeps the same character recognizable in every scene.

Step 7. Grade the color. Color Grading finishes the look with 50+ palettes and manual control over temperature, contrast, grain, and exposure. Grading at the project level keeps every scene in the same tonal world.

Assembling and Finishing the Video

The final step is arranging the generated scenes in story order and closing the gaps between them. Watch the scenes back to back in script order first: this is where pacing problems and missing beats become visible.

When a shot needs more room, Forward and Backward Extend continues a clip in either direction, so a scene can grow without regenerating it from scratch. If a transition between two scenes feels abrupt, an extended lead-in or lead-out on either side usually solves it.
