Creating food commercials with AI starts with the same fundamentals as any strong food ad. An appetizing hero shot, purposeful movement, detailed textures, and a clear visual concept still carry the whole ad. AI tools help turn those ideas into individual shots. Those shots come together into a complete commercial, built shot by shot instead of filmed in one take.

What Makes a Good AI Food Commercial

A food commercial works when it makes the product look like something worth reaching for right now, not just visible on screen. That means close, detailed textures on the food itself, movement that feels purposeful and not random, and lighting that makes ingredients look fresh, not flat. A drizzle of sauce, steam rising off a plate, condensation on a cold glass, these small details do more work than a wide shot of the finished dish sitting still.

The shots that make up a food commercial usually fall into a handful of recognizable types, each doing a different job in the final cut. A macro shot sells texture, a splash or pour shot sells freshness and motion, a hero shot sells the finished product as the payoff. Knowing which type a given moment in the ad calls for makes it easier to plan the shot list before generating anything.

Full Workflow: Step-by-Step Guide

Step 1: Start from a product photo. Upload a clear product photo or paste the product page URL to bring the product into Marketing Studio, a visual reference for packaging, colors, shape, and branding. 

Step 2: Define the ad's goal before picking shots. The hook, the product benefit or offer, the CTA, and where it'll run. A short social ad might need a strong opening moment, 2 to 3 appetite shots, then the product and a CTA, a product page video weights those pieces differently.

Step 3: Pick the shot types that goal needs. A hero shot, a macro texture shot, a splash or pour, steam, slow motion, whichever ones Step 2 calls for, from the table above.

Step 4: Pick a Marketing Studio format, or write a prompt for something more specific. Prompting Tips for Better Food Ads

Name the specific texture, not just the food: "glossy caramel drizzle" gets a different result than just "caramel."
Describe motion with a direction and a speed: "sauce pouring slowly from top left" works better than "pouring sauce."
Call out steam, condensation, or moisture explicitly if the shot needs to signal fresh or hot, these details rarely show up unless they're named.
Specify the camera distance for texture shots: "extreme close-up" reads differently than "close-up" and changes how much detail comes through.
Keep lighting consistent across shots by describing it the same way each time, a warm key light from one direction reads as one continuous ad, not several disconnected clips.
Reference the real product photo whenever the shot needs to match an actual package, color, or label exactly.

How to Adapt a Food Ad for Different Formats

The same set of generated shots doesn't have to lock into a single edit. Once the hero shot, the macro detail, the pour, and the product reveal exist as separate pieces, they can be arranged differently depending on where the ad is going to run and what it needs to communicate there.

Short social ad. A fast hook up front, one or two detail or motion shots, then the product reveal, cut tight enough to hold attention in a feed.
Product page video. More time spent on the product itself, packaging, texture, the exact details a shopper would want to see before buying.
Vertical ad. The composition and the motion need to work in 9:16 specifically, a pour or a splash framed for landscape won't sit right cropped into a vertical frame after the fact.
Marketplace creative. Keep the product as the clear, uncluttered subject of the frame, a busy or overly cinematic scene works against a listing image more than it helps.
Brand campaign. More room for cinematic shots and a visual story built around the product, not a fast, transactional cut.
