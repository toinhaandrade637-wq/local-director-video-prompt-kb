What Does "Built for Agencies" Mean in AI Video?

Team and enterprise plans are how AI video platforms serve companies: several seats on one invoice, a shared workspace, and admin controls on top of the same generation tools. Agency work adds requirements that go beyond what such a plan covers by default. Three of them come up in almost every setup conversation:

Separate client projects. Client A's assets, references, and drafts stay invisible to client B, and a freelancer gets access to one project with an end date while the rest of the account stays closed. A project inside a shared workspace organizes access; an independent workspace per client gives stronger separation, and platforms differ in which of the two they offer and on which tier.
Team seats with pooled billing. One invoice, one credit pool, roles for who can generate and who can only review, and spend controls per member.
Commercial terms that allow delivery. Rights apply on the tier the team actually uses, and the terms allow the agency to deliver or sublicense outputs to the client.

A team plan covers part of this picture on its own. The rest depends on how a platform structures workspaces, roles, and rights, which is why the plans below differ more in architecture than in price. For enterprise marketing teams choosing by brand governance, the criteria shift, and we cover that angle in the enterprise marketing video generators ranking.

Model Access: One Subscription or Several Vendor Plans

Model access is a practical criterion that rarely makes it into agency comparisons. A campaign brief in 2026 often calls for Kling for motion, Seedance for realism, Veo for dialogue scenes, and an image model for stills. A team buying those natively manages several vendor plans at once: Kling Team starts at 3 seats at $79 each, Runway Team runs $69 per seat, and HeyGen Business starts at $149 plus $20 per seat, each with its own credit pool, terms, and renewal date. Model availability changes quickly, so agencies confirm the current model list before choosing a plan: OpenAI discontinued the Sora app on April 26, 2026, and the Sora API follows on September 24, 2026, so briefs that called for it now route to other models.

One platform subscription with 30+ models brings the same brief into a single workspace: one credit pool, one invoice, and one rights document. On Higgsfield, Kling, Seedance, Veo, and Nano Banana run inside the same team workspace, so the agency manages several models through one credit balance and one invoice. The caveat: flagship access windows on a platform differ from native plans, and unlimited model access applies on higgsfield.ai, with MCP, CLI, Canvas, and Supercomputer running on plan credits. We compared the two purchasing routes in the all-in-one subscription breakdown.

Example: How Client Revisions Work in Higgsfield

By the third round of revisions, a brief usually changes in small ways: the client swaps the hero product, asks for a second language, and wants a vertical cut. With single-shot generation, each of those changes means a new render and a new review of everything that was already approved.

Genjutsu is how Higgsfield handles that cycle: it changes one element of a finished video, the product, the text, or the background, and helps preserve the approved footage, with every result still going through review before it returns to the client. We broke the workflow down in the editing without regenerating guide. Dubbing and reframing tools on higgsfield.ai then cover the language swap and the vertical version from the same asset, without a new generation.

Setting Up a New Client: a Six-Step Checklist
Create the client's space: a sub-workspace on Higgsfield Enterprise, a dedicated workspace on Runway, or a separate team workspace on Kling.
Set the folder structure by deliverable: ads, socials, drafts, approved.
