What Matters When You Pick AI Tools for a Short Film?
Pick tools that keep the same faces, places and look from shot to shot, because a short film is a sequence of shots that has to hold together.
Six things to check:
Reference input: how many images, videos or audio files you can feed in, and what they control (face, wardrobe, location, style).
Character and location consistency: whether the tool saves characters, locations and props for reuse across shots.
Camera and motion control: lens, framing and camera moves set as controls, not only described in a prompt.
Fixing single shots: extending a shot or changing one detail without generating the whole clip again.
Sound: dialogue, effects and music, generated with the shot or separately.
Handoff to the edit: export formats, editor plugins and how footage reaches your timeline.
Each tool below is judged on its own job. An editor is not marked down for not generating video, and an upscaler is not marked down for having no sound.

Real Example: A Two-Shot AI Scene With Dialogue and Sound
The scene below was made on Higgsfield from a single keyframe. One image held both actors’ faces, their wardrobe, the car and the gas station, while the prompt set the camera move, the focus pull, the dialogue and the sound. Picture and sound came out of one generation.

A short film is a sequence of shots that has to hold together, so the right AI tools keep the same faces, places and look from shot to shot. This guide picks one or two tools for each stage: LTX Studio and Midjourney for planning, Higgsfield, Runway and Luma for shots, ElevenLabs for sound, and Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve Studio and Topaz Video for the edit and finishing. It includes a real two-shot scene with dialogue and its full cost. It also suggests three ready-made toolkits.
What the scene shows:
Two shots in one clip: a long take that starts on an extreme macro of Danny lighting a cigarette, pulls back to a medium two-shot with a slow focus rack, then a hard cut to a shot from inside the car trunk.
Two characters who keep the same faces and wardrobe through the move.
Lip-synced dialogue and ambient sound (lighter, passing truck, trunk lid) generated with the picture.
Prompt (excerpt):
 “One continuous take on a 50mm: an extreme macro in left profile on Danny’s face as the lighter’s flame touches the cigarette, then the camera pulls back on a slider to a medium two-shot and the focus racks slowly to Sal walking out of the kiosk. Hard cut: an 18mm wide lying flat on the trunk floor, lens pointing up. Early-1990s American crime drama, practical light only, lip-synced dialogue, no music.”
Settings:
 the keyframe was made with Soul Cinema on Higgsfield, and the video with Seedance 2.5 on Higgsfield in References mode with that one image, at 16:9, 1080p and 15 seconds, with High bitrate and sound on.

**Color:** 60:30:10 — black-blue night / warm amber on skin and wet asphalt / cool teal-green of the canopy tubes and streetlights. Exactly the grade of @[Image 1](image_1).
**Camera:** Physical spherical cine lenses, 180-degree shutter motion blur. Danny: the two small hoops in his left ear (lobe and upper cartilage) and the faded tattoo on his neck below the left ear, exactly as in @[Image 1](image_1). How We Chose These Tools
We picked one or two tools for each stage of a short film: planning, shots, sound, editing and enhancement. Each tool had to be in active development in 2026, publish its prices, and offer a paid plan with commercial use. Prices come from each tool’s official pricing page on October 30, 2026, at the regular monthly rate. Annual prices and promotions are listed separately where they apply.
The tools are grouped by film stage. Within the shot-generation group, Higgsfield comes first because it covers several stages, from characters and shots to sound and the editor handoff.
LTX Studio: Best for Storyboarding and Shot Planning
LTX Studio turns a script into scenes and a storyboard, then lets you generate shots from those boards in the same project. It is built for planning a film before you spend credits on finished shots, with saved characters, locations and objects that keep the boards consistent.
Best for:
 a solo filmmaker turning a script into a board and a first animatic.
What’s inside:
 script upload that generates scenes and storyboards, Elements for saved characters, objects and locations, and camera controls with keyframes on every plan. LTX-2.5 and LTX-2.3 run on all plans, Kling 3.0 and Seedance 2.0 are added from Standard, and Veo 3.1 on Pro. For a wider look at planning tools, see our guide to AI previsualization tools.
Price and limits:
 Lite costs $15/month for 8,000 credits and Standard $35/month for 28,000 credits. The free plan gives 800 one-time credits for personal use. Annual billing is 20% off.
Where it falls short:
 the commercial use license starts on Standard, so the $15 Lite plan removes the watermark but does not list commercial use. AI storyboards and saved Elements also start on Standard, and the plan pages do not list an export format for Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve.
Midjourney: Best for Visual Style and Reference Frames
Midjourney is an image generator that many filmmakers use for look development: mood frames, costume and location ideas, and reference stills that later drive video generation. V8.2 has been the default since July 2026, renders HD images and keeps style references consistent with V7.
Best for:
 a director building a lookbook and keyframes before generating shots.
What’s inside:
 V8.1 as the default model with an HD mode, plus style references and personalization that hold one look across frames. Omni Reference (V7) puts a specific character, object or vehicle into an image. Video generation runs in SD on Basic and in SD and HD from Standard. The stills work as references in Higgsfield, Runway or Luma.
Price and limits:
 Basic costs $10/month with 3.3 hours of fast GPU time; Standard at $30/month adds unlimited Relax image generation. Annual billing is 20% off, or $8/month for Basic.
Where it falls short:
 there are no script, storyboard or timeline tools, so it covers the look, not the sequence. Stealth mode, which keeps your images private, is only on Pro and Mega, and companies with more than $1M in annual revenue must use one of those two plans.
Higgsfield: Best for Character-Consistent Shots
Higgsfield is a generative platform for creative production, with 35+ frontier models for generating images and turning them into video scenes. Its Cinema Studio 4.0 sets camera, lens, light and color in a Director’s Panel, takes up to 50 references and generates clips up to 30 seconds with sound.
Best for:
 a solo filmmaker who needs the same characters across many shots and a directed camera.
What’s inside:
 Cinema Studio 4.0 offers camera bodies, lenses, 30+ movement presets, lighting and 50+ color templates, with clips up to 30 seconds at up to 1080p. You can extend a scene or change one detail with Edit Video without a new generation. AI Cast creates AI actors, Elements saves characters, locations and props, and Kling 3.0 on Higgsfield makes 2 to 6 shots in one generation. Seed Audio 1.0, Eleven v4, Topaz and ByteDance upscalers and plugins for Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve sit in the same account. For a step-by-step walk-through of one scene, see 
How to Master AI Filmmaking.
Price and limits:
 plans start at $15/month for 200 credits, with commercial use on all paid plans. Generations, extensions and edits are billed in credits, and credits for failed generations usually return automatically.
Where it falls short:
 your own footage goes in through the editor plugins or Genjutsu, which changes part of the frame in an uploaded 4 to 30 second video.
Runway: Best for Editing Filmed Footage
Runway is a video generation and editing platform with its own Gen-4.5 model, the Aleph 2.0 video-to-video editor and third-party models such as Kling 4.0 and Seedance 2.5. It works on both generated clips and filmed footage, which suits films that mix the two.
Best for:
 a filmmaker changing weather, props or light in shots they already filmed.
What’s inside:
 Gen-4.5 at 12 credits per second, or 60 credits for 5 seconds, and Aleph 2.0 for video-to-video editing of existing clips. Act-Two captures performance with head, face, body and hand tracking. Standard adds 4K upscaling, and Max adds ProRes, HDR and image sequence export for an editor such as DaVinci Resolve Studio.
Price and limits:
 Standard costs $15/month for 625 credits and Pro $35/month for 2,250 credits. The free plan gives 125 one-time credits. Annual billing lowers Standard to $12/month.
Where it falls short:
 monthly credits on Standard and Pro do not roll over, while Max rolls over up to one month. Standard’s 625 credits cover about 52 seconds of Gen-4.5 a month, and ProRes, HDR and image sequence export start on Max at $95/month.
Luma: Best for HDR Shots and Grading-Ready Exports
Luma runs its Ray3.2 model, released in June 2026, alongside models such as Kling 4.0, Veo 3.1 and Seedance 2.5. Ray3.2 generates native HDR video and exports 16-bit EXR files, which gives a colorist more room to grade than standard video files.
Best for:
 a filmmaker who grades in DaVinci Resolve and wants generated shots that hold up in the grade.
What’s inside:
 Ray3.2 clips up to 20 seconds at 1080p, with up to 16 keyframes in one clip, native HDR and 16-bit EXR export in ACES2065-1. Reframe changes the aspect ratio, extends the frame or replaces a background, and performance tracking follows up to eight faces. The EXR files go into DaVinci Resolve Studio for HDR grading.
Price and limits:
 Plus costs $30/month for 10,000 credits, enough for 25 five-second Ray3.2 clips at 1080p; Pro costs $90/month for 40,000 credits. Commercial use starts on Plus, and annual billing lowers Plus to $25/month.
Where it falls short:
 Ray3.2 generates video without sound, so dialogue and effects need a tool such as ElevenLabs. 1080p costs four times as much as 720p (400 versus 100 credits for 5 seconds), HDR doubles the base cost and HDR with EXR triples it.
ElevenLabs: Best for Voice Cloning and Sound Effects
ElevenLabs is an audio platform for voices, sound effects, music and dubbing. Its Eleven v4 model speaks 90+ languages with multi-speaker dialogue and audio tags such as whispers or laughs, and its Studio puts voice, music and effects on one timeline over your video.
Best for:
 a filmmaker voicing several characters or dubbing a short into other languages.
What’s inside:
 Eleven v4 with multi-speaker dialogue and audio tags, instant voice cloning from Starter and Professional Voice Cloning from Creator. Sound effects run up to 30 seconds, Music v2 exports stems, and Studio syncs voiceovers, music and effects over MP4 or MOV files, next to Dubbing Studio. Higgsfield already has Eleven v4 and Seed Audio 1.0 for sound made with the shot; the ElevenLabs app adds voice cloning, a sound timeline, music stems and dubbing.
Price and limits:
 Starter costs $6/month for 30,000 credits, about 30 minutes of speech, with a commercial license; Creator costs $22/month. The free plan is non-commercial.
Where it falls short:
 sound effects cap at 30 seconds per generation, and Eleven v4 takes up to 5,000 characters per generation. Music on Starter is licensed for commercial use but not for streaming.
Adobe Premiere Pro: Best for Editing With AI Search
Premiere Pro is Adobe’s timeline editor with AI tools for the jobs editors repeat most: finding clips, transcribing, cleaning dialogue and stretching a shot that ends too early. Firefly generation of video and sound effects sits inside the timeline and runs on generative credits.
Best for:
 an editor who already cuts in Premiere and wants AI shots and fixes without leaving the timeline.
What’s inside:
 Generative Extend adds frames and ambient sound to a short clip, and Media Intelligence finds clips by plain-language description. Speech to Text supports text-based editing, Enhance Speech cleans dialogue, captions translate into 18 languages, and Object Mask tracks a subject. The Higgsfield plugin for Premiere Pro 2025 and newer adds generation, Edit Video and upscaling on the timeline, using the same credits as on the web.
Price and limits:
 $22.99/month on an annual plan billed monthly; Creative Cloud Pro costs $69.99/month. There is a 7-day free trial.
Where it falls short:
 the single-app plan includes 25 generative credits a month, against 4,000 on Creative Cloud Pro, and credit costs per AI feature are not listed on the plans page. Premiere is subscription only, with no perpetual license.
DaVinci Resolve Studio: Best for Color and Finishing in One App
DaVinci Resolve is Blackmagic Design’s editor, color, audio and effects suite in one app. Version 21 includes AI tools such as IntelliScript, which builds a cut from a screenplay. The paid Studio version adds the Neural Engine features, higher resolutions and HDR grading.
Best for:
 a filmmaker who wants one app for the cut, the grade and the sound mix, paid for once.
What’s inside:
 Neural Engine tools such as Magic Mask, Speed Warp, Super Scale, AI UltraNR noise reduction and Smart Reframe, and Fairlight audio with Voice Isolation, Dialogue Separator and Music Remixer. Studio handles up to 32K and 120 fps, 10-bit formats, Dolby Vision and HDR10+ grading. The Higgsfield plugin for DaVinci Resolve 19 and newer adds generation, Edit Video, upscaling, Cinema Studio and AI LUT Creator inside Resolve; see Higgsfield Meets DaVinci Resolve.
Price and limits:
 $295 one-time license, on sale for $236 when we checked; the free version costs nothing. Multi-user work runs through Blackmagic Cloud.
Where it falls short:
 most Neural Engine AI tools are Studio-only, and the free version stops at Ultra HD and 60 fps with 8-bit formats. No text-to-video generation is listed among Resolve 21’s AI tools.
Topaz Video: Best for Upscaling and Frame Interpolation
Topaz Video is a desktop app for Mac and Windows that enhances finished footage: upscaling to 4K, removing noise, stabilizing the camera, creating new frames for smooth slow motion and converting SDR to HDR. It runs locally without limits, with monthly cloud credits for its newest models.
Best for:
 a filmmaker finishing a mix of AI shots and low-light or archival footage.
What’s inside:
 more than 20 enhancement models, including Starlight, Proteus, Iris, Nyx and Rhea, for upscaling, denoising, frame interpolation, slow motion, stabilization, motion deblur and SDR to HDR. It runs as a standalone app or as a plugin for DaVinci Resolve and After Effects, with a Premiere panel. Higgsfield has Topaz and ByteDance upscalers built in for AI shots; the desktop app adds denoising, stabilization, interpolation and unlimited local rendering for any footage, including what you filmed.
Price and limits:
 $59/month or $299/year, with 25 cloud credits a month that do not roll over; Topaz Video Pro costs $74/month with an annual commitment.
Where it falls short:
 standard plans allow commercial use only for organizations under $1M in annual revenue, so larger companies need Topaz Video Pro. The desktop app runs on Mac and Windows only.
What Does an AI Short Film Cost?
Most of the cost is credits per shot plus retakes: our 15-second 1080p scene took 240 credits per generation on Higgsfield, and we used two video attempts. The tools charge in three ways: credits that run down per generation (Higgsfield, Runway, Luma, LTX Studio), subscriptions with usage limits (Midjourney, ElevenLabs, Premiere Pro, Topaz Video) and a one-time license (DaVinci Resolve Studio). Most free plans are for personal use only.

Can one AI tool make a whole short film?
No. Higgsfield covers several stages in one account, from characters and shots to sound and upscaling, but you still plan the shots, choose takes and cut the film.
How much does an AI short film cost?
It depends on length and retakes. Our 15-second 1080p scene cost 240 credits per generation on Higgsfield, about $11.76. A two-minute film from eight such clips needs about 1,920 credits before retakes.
Can I use AI-generated footage commercially?
Yes, on most paid plans. Higgsfield allows it on all paid plans, ElevenLabs from Starter, Luma from Plus and LTX Studio from its $35 plan. Free plans are usually personal use only.
Which AI tools work inside Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve?
Higgsfield has plugins for both, with generation, Edit Video and upscaling on the timeline. Topaz Video has a plugin for Resolve and a panel for Premiere.
How do I keep the same character across shots?
Create the character once and reuse it as a reference: AI Cast and Elements on Higgsfield, Elements in LTX Studio, character references in Luma and Runway.
