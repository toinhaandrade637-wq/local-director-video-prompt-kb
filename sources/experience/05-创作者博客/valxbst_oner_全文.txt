Step 1. Two plates, one suit

He has a day self and a night self, and the cheap way to build that is not two costumes. It is one costume in two states.

Both plates are built on the same locked identity plate, and the second one opens its wardrobe paragraph by naming the first:

He wears the same suit as his daytime look, stripped back and disordered.

That sentence is the whole trick. Without it the model builds a second outfit, and then you have two men who happen to look alike. With it you get one man at two times of day, which is the entire idea of the film.

Both plates share the same opening: a flat 18% neutral grey field, flat shadowless light, zero shadow and zero light bleed outside the silhouette. A wardrobe plate that carries lighting of its own will fight the lighting of every scene you drop it into. It should carry the garment and nothing else.

The shape, if you want to reuse it: the CRITICALs sit at the top, because the model holds the top hardest. Then ASSETS — what each reference controls, and only that — then GEOMETRY MAP, FIRST FRAME, CAMERA, LIGHT, COLOUR, ATMOSPHERE, then ACTION TIMING as a clock, then PHYSICS, ACTING, AUDIO, and a LOCKS paragraph at the bottom that restates every non-negotiable in one place.

Lighting lives in its own block above the timing rather than inside the beats. Word count is screen-time weighting whether you intend it or not, so anything that isn't an event stays out of the schedule.

Why a double life is the right story for a oner

This piece landed on the first roll. The one after it took four. The difference was not the prompt — it was the premise.

A oner can cross space or time. Not both.

Six locations here, all one city, all the same night, all walkable in an evening. The model holds a single lighting state for thirty seconds because there is a single state to hold. Ask an unbroken take to cross 500km and a sunset and you have asked for something physically impossible; the model will resolve that contradiction somewhere, and it will not ask you where.

Two more things this premise gets for free.

The locations are generic. A konbini aisle, a ramen counter, a parking garage. Enormous training data, interchangeable, and — the real point — nothing for a viewer to check them against. Drift doesn't read. Famous, specific places make every deviation visible.

The ending is a physical event. A man pulling a tie loose, taking a jacket off, swapping a shoe. Physical events survive generation. Two small hand movements carrying emotional weight do not.

And the form matches the idea: montage-as-oner is what a double life feels like. When the premise justifies the technique, the technique stops fighting you.

The timing mistake

The rooftop is the weakest part of the film. The camera is too fast and the man moves too fast, and it looks like the model is bad at physical acting.

It isn't. It's arithmetic, and it's mine.

Five transformation stages scheduled into nine seconds is 1.8 seconds per stage — including the camera flight between them. Taking a suit jacket off takes about four seconds. The beat was impossible as written, so the model did the only thing left: it sped the man up to fit the schedule, and the camera with him.

Budget human action time before you write the beat. A costume change or any physical action needs 3–4 seconds of screen time minimum, plus its transition. Three stages in nine seconds, or five in fifteen. Count it — don't feel it.

Three would have read better anyway.

Cutting by measurement, not by eye

The version that shipped is 25.5 seconds, not 30. The cut is the fix for the timing error: it removes the two fastest stages.

The cut point was measured rather than eyeballed. Frame-to-frame difference across 23.0–26.0s shows the dead stop holding flat from 24.25 to 24.63 — then the next camera throw explodes at 24.667 and peaks at 25.0. So the cut goes at 24.64: the last calm frame of the hold, not the first frame of the move.

Cutting mid-move looks like a mistake. Cutting on the last still frame of a deliberate stop looks like an ending.

Then that frame is frozen for 0.85s to give the ending its beat back:

ffmpeg -i 30s.mp4 -t 24.64 -c copy part1.mp4
ffmpeg -i 30s.mp4 -ss 24.60 -frames:v 1 last.png
ffmpeg -loop 1 -i last.png -t 0.85 -r 24 -pix_fmt yuv420p freeze.mp4
printf "file 'part1.mp4'\nfile 'freeze.mp4'\n" > cc.txt
ffmpeg -f concat -safe 0 -i cc.txt -c copy out.mp4

It also loops cleanly back to the office orbit, which matters more on a vertical feed than anything else in this section.




Three things worth knowing

One garment, two states — never two costumes. Name the day look inside the night look's own wardrobe line and describe the difference as condition, not as clothing. Two separate wardrobe descriptions give you two separate men.

"Empty" has to include the reflections. Dark monitors, night windows and wet asphalt are mirrors. If the emptiness clause only covers the room, the extra person appears in the glass.

"No music" needs a vocabulary list. No BGM on its own does very little. What worked was naming the whole category: no score, no soundtrack, no instrumental, no underscore, no ambient pad, no drone, no tone bed, no swell, no sting, no humming, no singing, no lyrics — diegetic sound only. Even then, check the returned audio and strip it at assembly if a bed rode along. The generator reports success on the request regardless.
