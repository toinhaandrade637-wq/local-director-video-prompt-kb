Step 3: Make the power plant

Generate the hall, then darken it to night in any photo editor: exposure down, a bit less colour. This is the most important step. Lightning and fire only look real when the world around them is dark, so the effects are the brightest thing in the shot and blow the camera out. In a bright hall they look pasted on.

ONE real photograph of a real place at night, a single full-frame view: no grid, no panels, no people, no text, no labels.




THE PLACE: the generator hall of an old 1920s brick power plant in a big city, at night. A long, WIDE hall with soot-blackened brick walls, tall brick arches, riveted steel tie-beams and small arched windows high up.




THE RECEIVERS: along the LEFT wall stands a row of five identical receivers about three metres apart, lined up side by side like a row of cannons, every one aimed straight across the hall at the yellow line. Each is a heavy machine on a black cast-iron stand: a long horizontal body of ribbed dark-brown glazed porcelain insulator rings, about two metres long, at waist height. Its BACK end is against the left wall, where thick braided copper cables drop into an iron conduit in the floor. Its FRONT end faces the yellow line and carries a pitted, scorched copper ball the size of a football.




THE GAP (the most important part): between the copper balls and the yellow line lies a long, wide stretch of bare, scorched concrete, SIX METRES across, about three times a man's height: much wider than the walkway behind the line, the widest open space in the picture. Nothing stands on it. Black scorch streaks run across it from the line toward each copper ball, and each ball has a burnt black star on the floor beneath it. The yellow line is far from the receivers: anyone standing on it would be a long way from the nearest copper ball.




THE YELLOW LINE: one worn, painted yellow line on the scuffed concrete, running down the length of the hall parallel to the row of receivers, on the far side of THE GAP. This is where a lightning bender stands, facing his own receiver's copper ball across the gap.




THE WALKWAY AND RIGHT-HAND WALL: behind the yellow line, a three-metre walkway, then the right-hand brick wall: a panel of big round analogue gauges set into an arched wooden door, a large round wall clock with a white face and black hands, steel welding visors hanging on pegs on a wooden rail, wooden crates, and a long, low, worn wooden bench against the wall. THE CAMERA: a wide view at standing eye height from the walkway close to the right-hand wall, looking diagonally down the hall and across THE GAP. HOW THINGS LOOK

THE LIGHTNING POSE (the power-plant workers' pose): a long, strong lunge, side-on to the receiver, right foot forward, the front knee bent and the back leg straight, torso upright; the right arm straight out at shoulder height toward the copper ball with the index and middle fingers together; the left arm stretched straight back behind, both arms in one line through the shoulders. One hand points.

THE FOREMAN'S BOLT: from THE LIGHTNING POSE behind the yellow line, a crackling white-hot bolt of lightning glowing blue, as thick as a finger, leaps level out of his two right fingertips across the six metres of floor into the copper ball, joining his hand to the ball like the arc between two electrodes. It stays connected, its jagged path re-forming every frame, short forks along it all reaching toward the ball; the ball flares white where it hits. LIGHT FROM ELECTRICITY: it blows out the picture and flickers blue-white over him, the receiver and the floor between them.

LIGHT FROM ELECTRICITY (every time): while any electricity is on, it is the brightest thing in the hall and its light comes from its whole length: cold blue-white light that flickers rapidly and unevenly, like a welding arc, very bright one frame and darker the next, with hard shadows that jitter. It lights the person making it, the receiver and the floor between them, falling off quickly with distance, and it hits the lens: a soft glare washes across the image with each flicker and the exposure jumps. Its core blows out to pure white with a blue glow around it. No steady glow, no spotlight, no floodlit room.

THE READY STANCE (the young man's firebending stance): behind the yellow line, side-on to his receiver, feet a long stride apart with the right foot forward, the front knee bent over the toes and the back leg straight, weight balanced between both feet, torso upright and relaxed, chin level; both fists up, the right fist forward at chest height and the left fist at his ribs. Balanced and strong, the stance of a trained martial artist.

SETTLING IN (how the young man starts every attempt, about two and a half seconds, unhurried and with real weight, the way a trained martial artist starts a form): standing behind the yellow line facing his receiver, he draws one slow breath in through his nose; as he breathes out, his right foot slides forward along the concrete with a soft scuff, his weight sinks onto his bent front knee, he shifts his back foot once to get it right, and his arms rise into place. Then he holds the stance for a breath, focused, his chest rising and falling, eyes on the copper ball across the floor.

THE ATTEMPT FRAMING (how every attempt is filmed): the friend stands on the walkway about six metres from the young man, close to the right-hand wall, looking down the hall at a slight angle. The young man is whole, head to sneakers, side-on in the right half of the frame, behind the yellow line, facing LEFT; the yellow line runs away down the hall past his feet; across the six metres of bare floor, on the left of the frame, his receiver aims its copper ball back at him, and the rest of the row recedes down the hall behind it.

REAL FIRE (every time): plain orange-yellow campfire flame with dark smoky edges, no blue, ragged and gone in a moment. It lights whoever made it orange for an instant; the exposure blows orange, then recovers. No glow left on the skin; the hand it comes from never burns.

THE FIREBENDER PUNCH: from THE READY STANCE, a sharp exhale through the mouth and ONE straight punch with his right fist toward the copper ball; his left fist stays at his ribs. A short burst of REAL FIRE leaps out just in front of his knuckles, about an arm's length, and burns out in the air within half a second, far short of the receiver; for that instant the picture floods orange and blows out, then recovers.
