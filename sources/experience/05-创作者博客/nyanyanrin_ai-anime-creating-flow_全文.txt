Here are the prompts you’ve been asking for!

I wanted to share my AI anime workflow for if it was me. In this post, you’ll find how I approach the story, shots, and character movement, along with a few prompt examples you can try.

My goal is to create animation with natural emotions and a cinematic feel. There are a few details I pay particular attention to, so I’ll use the two scenes from this project to walk you through them.




1. Start with the story and character design sheets

First, I work out the relationships between the characters and how their emotions change throughout each scene.

In the first scene, the girl gathers the courage to confess her feelings. After being rejected, she discovers that her childhood friend witnessed everything. Her emotions shift from hope to hurt, then embarrassment and defensiveness.

In the second scene, she initially resists his attempt to get close, before finally breaking down in his arms. He comforts her while keeping his own feelings hidden.




Character design sheets

So, I prepare the character design sheets first, establishing consistent hairstyles, hair colors, outfits, and distinctive features. Along with front views, side and back views are useful for keeping the characters consistent across different shots.

For me, knowing what a character is thinking gives their expressions and movements a clear direction.







2. Break the story into clear shots

For every shot, I want to know who the audience should be looking at, what they should notice, and which emotion the shot should convey.

During the confession, for example, I use a close-up of the girl with Boy 2’s hair out of focus in the foreground. This keeps the attention on her expression as she waits for his answer.

Character entrances and movements also need a clear sequence. For Boy 1’s entrance around the corner, I can describe it like this:

HARD CUT to the corridor corner.

SHOT 2 — 0:04.2–0:06.4

Medium shot tightening into the childhood friend’s reaction through composition.

He arrives at the corner and stops abruptly after hearing the confession.

His lips part as if he had been about to call her name. He immediately closes his mouth and retreats behind the wall.

His hand tightens around his schoolbag strap.

He looks down, hesitates, then cautiously looks around the corner.




HARD CUT to the camera holds on boy 1 near profile and shoulder. 

SHOT 3 — 0:06.4–0:10.2 

The girl and Boy 2 remain softly out of focus behind him. Boy 1 grips his schoolbag strap and keeps still, listening.

This separates the action into distinct steps: walking out, noticing, turning away, and stepping aside. It helps communicate that he needs to enter the frame, rather than already be standing there when the shot begins.

That’s something I always check: how did the character get into this position? The beginning, progression, and end of the movement should all be clear on screen.










I also explicitly write HARD CUT between each shot.

My aim is to cut directly to the next composition without having the AI invent an unwanted transition, such as the camera suddenly orbiting a character, the background sliding around, or a character rotating like a 3D model.

That unnatural, 3D-like movement is exactly what I want to avoid in this style of 2D anime. It can interrupt both the animated feel and the emotion of the scene.

Along with HARD CUT, I specify what the camera does within each shot. If it should stay still, I write “fixed framing.” I only ask for movement where I actually want a follow shot or a pullback.

Use fixed framing and crisp hard cuts between shots. Cut on a glance, a reaction, or a decisive movement. Avoid automatic camera orbiting, floating camera moves, and 3D-like rotational transitions.

3. Express emotion through close-ups and small movements

The girl’s line, 「……そっか。」—“…I see.”—is one of my favorite examples.

It’s a very short line, but the framing and movement can show her trying to accept the answer before she has fully processed how she feels.

I frame it as an extreme side close-up. She slowly lowers her head, and her fringe hides her eyes, leaving her mouth, cheek, and front hair strands visible. As she speaks, a fine strand slips downward with the movement of her head, then gently settles.

Here’s how I describe it in the prompt:

Extreme side close-up of the girl’s lower face. She slowly lowers her head, and her fringe obscures her eyes.

Girl:「……そっか。」

As she speaks, one fine front strand slips slightly lower beside her cheek, remaining attached, then gently settles. Her mouth relaxes after the line. Keep her cheeks dry and hold briefly before cutting.

She isn’t crying yet. Her mouth losing its composure, her head slowly lowering, and the hair settling a little later already communicate her disappointment.

That’s the moment I want to hold onto: she is still trying to keep herself together. Her tears only fall after Boy 2 leaves, giving the emotion room to build.




4. Build a cinematic feel through lighting and atmosphere

For this story, I chose warm after-school sunlight paired with deeper, cooler shadows to give the characters clear light-and-shadow definition.

I concentrate the soft glow around the hair, eyes, and illuminated edges while keeping facial features, linework, and shadows readable. A few sparkling particles can add depth, but the expressions should remain clear.

Soft cinematic glow with strong contrast between warm sunlight and cool shadows. Preserve clear facial details, deep shadow areas, and a delicate rim light along the hair.

I really like the combination of soft light and defined shadows. It gives the image a gentle quality while preserving its emotional weight.

At the end of the second scene, the background gradually fades into white, leaving only the two characters embracing. The attention stays on that embrace and the feelings the boy never says out loud.




5. Make the animation expressive while keeping a sense of weight

When designing movement, I like to remind myself:

“What makes 2D anime different from live action is those movements people wouldn’t normally make in real life.”

For me, this means animation can amplify everyday reactions through more expressive poses, facial expressions, and timing, making an emotion immediately readable.

For example, Boy 2 firmly presses his palms together and gives a wink. That slightly exaggerated apology reveals how casually he brushes off the situation.




The girl gripping Boy 1’s shirt, saying “It’s so frustrating,” and then breaking into loud sobs is another clear sequence of emotional actions.

These movements need supporting physical reactions. When her fingers tighten, the shirt gathers into folds. When a head turns, the hair follows a moment later.

For hair physics, I might include:

The head leads the movement. Larger hair sections follow, then the tips. Use a small overshoot, a gentle rebound, and natural settling.

The follow-through, rebound, and gradual settling are small details I care about. They help exaggerated movement retain a natural sense of weight.




6. Leave room for pauses so the audience can feel the hesitation

Another detail I want to share is simply giving the characters a moment to wait.

In the second scene, after Boy 1 sits down, the girl pauses before moving away. When he notices the distance she has created, he also waits before approaching her.

Those two pauses give the audience time to read her defensiveness, along with his hesitation and concern.

After he sits, hold a quiet beat before she shifts away. He waits through another pause before moving closer. Keep both pauses clearly visible.

Sound should support that timing too. His breathing after running, her sleeves brushing against her face, the fabric moving as she grips his shirt, and her eventual crying all make the emotions feel more tangible.










I also clearly label his thoughts as “internal voice-over” and specify that his mouth stays still. This distinguishes what he says aloud from what he keeps inside.










After generating the footage, I check the character designs, positions, action sequences, dialogue, and emotional pacing. Then I adjust the parts of the prompt that weren’t clear enough. Each detail I describe more precisely brings the result closer to what I want the story to express.
