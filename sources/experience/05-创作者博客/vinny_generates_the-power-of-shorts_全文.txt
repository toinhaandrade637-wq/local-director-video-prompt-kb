Why I Stopped Trying to Make Every Short Perfect
How consistent short-form content helped me reach 10K YouTube subscribers, 5.5M views, 11K Instagram followers, and why imperfection can sometimes work in your favor

For a long time, I approached every AI video the same way.

If I noticed a small continuity issue, I wanted to regenerate it. If a movement looked slightly off, I wanted to fix it. If the physics were not completely accurate, I felt like the video was not ready to publish.




That mindset makes sense when you are creating a client film, a portfolio piece or a larger cinematic project.

But eventually I realized something:




Short-form content plays by different rules.




A Short does not always need to be your most polished piece of work.

Sometimes its job is simply to stop someone from scrolling.

Sometimes it needs to create curiosity.

Sometimes it needs to make someone laugh, react or argue.




And sometimes a tiny imperfection can actually become part of the reason people engage with it.

That change in mindset has had a major impact on the way I create content.

In just 28 days, my YouTube content generated 5,547,030 views while my channel grew to around 10,000 subscribers.




During that same period, my Instagram reached around 11,000 followers and generated approximately 10 million views.




One of the biggest factors behind that growth was simple:

I stopped treating every Short like a film festival entry.

Short-form content has a different purpose

There is still a place for perfection.

If I am producing a commercial, a long-form AI film or an important portfolio project, I want the character consistency to be right. I want the physics to work. I want the edit to feel intentional.

But Shorts are often much closer to experiments.

You are testing an idea against a real audience.




Does the first second make people stop?

Does the character make them curious?

Does the concept create tension?

Do people want to see what happens next?

Does someone watch it twice because they missed something?




These questions can be more important than whether every frame is technically perfect.

And that changes how much time I am willing to spend on an individual Short.




If a 20-second video already communicates the idea clearly, looks strong and has a good hook, spending another six hours fixing something that 95% of viewers will never notice may not always be the best decision.




Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it isn't.

Learning the difference is part of becoming a better creator.

Consistency gave me more opportunities to win

One of the biggest reasons I started growing faster was volume.

I began uploading roughly 2 to 4 Shorts per day.




That does not mean randomly uploading low-quality content.

It means building a workflow where I can consistently:

Create → Publish → Analyze → Improve → Repeat

Every upload becomes another test.




Another opportunity for YouTube, Instagram or another platform to place your content in front of someone who has never seen your work before.

And importantly, not every video needs to explode.




Some videos perform normally.

Some underperform.

Some surprise you.




And every once in a while, one suddenly reaches hundreds of thousands — or millions — of people.




If I spent a week perfecting every Short, I would dramatically reduce the number of opportunities I have to discover those breakout ideas.




In just 28 days, that consistent approach contributed to:

YouTube

10K subscribers
5,547,030 views

Instagram

11K followers
~10M views in 28 days

Those results did not come from one perfect upload.

They came from repeatedly giving myself another chance.

A small AI mistake can become engagement

AI video still produces imperfections.

Objects may move in unexpected ways.

Continuity can shift.

Physics might not be completely believable.

Background details can change.

An object might suddenly appear in a slightly different position between shots.

For larger productions, I would normally regenerate those moments.

But for short-form content, I sometimes make a different decision.

If the mistake does not destroy the scene and is not immediately distracting, I may leave it in.

And something interesting happens.

People notice.

Sometimes after watching the video once.

Sometimes after watching it twice.

Then they go to the comments.

Someone writes:

“That doesn't make sense.”

Someone else notices a different detail.

Another person responds:

“Obviously this is AI.”

Then someone starts explaining exactly what they think went wrong.

Before you know it, viewers are discussing the video with each other.

From a creator perspective, something that could have been seen purely as a flaw suddenly creates:

more comments;
more rewatches;
more discussion;
more attention;
more profile visits;
more engagement.

That does not mean intentionally making bad videos.

That distinction is extremely important.

Imperfect does not mean low quality

A weak concept does not become strong because it contains mistakes.

Bad visuals do not magically become viral because people criticize them.

Poor storytelling is still poor storytelling.




But there is a difference between a bad video and a small imperfection inside an otherwise strong video.




Sometimes that small imperfection gives the viewer another reason to interact.

Maybe they replay the video because something looked strange.

Maybe they pause the video.

Maybe they comment to point it out.

Maybe another viewer disagrees with them.




That interaction is valuable.

The goal should never be:

“How can I make this video worse?”




The better question is:

“Does this mistake actually matter enough to spend more time and resources fixing it?”

The real cost of perfection

This becomes even more important when working with generative video.

Every regeneration costs something.




Credits.

Time.

Processing.

Creative energy.




And sometimes fixing one small issue creates another issue somewhere else.

If you spend all of those resources trying to perfect every 15-second Short, you may actually reduce the amount of content you are able to publish.




For a major film?

Spend the credits.




For client work?

Fix the problem.




For an important portfolio piece?

Polish it.




But for a fast-moving piece of short-form content?

Sometimes publishing is more valuable than another ten generations.

Shorts are one of the fastest feedback systems available

Every Short teaches you something.

You learn which opening frames work.

You learn which characters people remember.

You discover whether viewers care about a particular storyline.

You find out which camera movements get attention.

You see which concepts create comments.

You learn where viewers become confused.

You discover which ideas people want to see again.

You learn what they want next.




That information is extremely valuable.

But you only receive it after publishing.




A video sitting unfinished on your computer gives you no audience data.

At some point, another hour of polishing becomes less valuable than pressing upload.

Shorts should not exist in isolation

This is probably one of the most important parts of my current strategy.

Short-form content becomes much more powerful when it is connected to something larger.




A Short can be the final piece of content.

But it can also be the entry point into your content ecosystem.

This is where long-form content becomes especially powerful.




I recently created a longer YouTube video breaking down how I made one of my MAY: Avalanche sequences.

Instead of letting that long-form video exist on its own, I can turn parts of it into multiple Shorts.

For example:

the final avalanche sequence;
the character reference breakdown;
the prompt structure;
camera direction;
the physics system;
continuity;
mistakes in the final generation;
audience comments;
the results and analytics.

One long-form video can suddenly produce multiple pieces of short-form content.

Long-form can feed Shorts

This is something I think more creators should take advantage of.

You do not always have to sit down and invent a completely new Short.

If you already spent hours creating a long-form YouTube video, there are probably multiple moments inside it that can become standalone clips.

A seven-minute tutorial might contain:

5, 10 or even 15 potential Shorts.

You can take the strongest hook from the video and make it its own clip.

For example:

“This AI video reached 3.9 million views — and I intentionally left some mistakes in it.”

That works perfectly as a Short.

You then give viewers enough information to understand the idea, but not necessarily everything.

At the end:




“I explain the full workflow in my YouTube video — link in bio.”




Now that Short has two purposes.

It generates reach.

And it sends viewers toward your long-form content.

Shorts can feed long-form too

The relationship works in both directions.

Sometimes I create the Short first.

If that Short performs extremely well, that is valuable information.

It tells me:

People are interested in this subject.

That means I can potentially turn it into something larger.

A viral MAY clip can become:

a full breakdown;
a behind-the-scenes video;
a prompt tutorial;
a longer MAY episode;
a filmmaking experiment;
a complete story.

This creates a cycle:

SHORT

Test the idea.

↓

AUDIENCE RESPONSE

See what people react to.

↓

LONG-FORM

Develop the strongest idea further.

↓

NEW SHORTS

Cut the long-form video into new pieces.

↓

DISCOVERY

Bring new people into the channel.

↓

LONG-FORM VIEWERS

Send them deeper into your content.

And then the cycle starts again.

Think in content ecosystems, not individual uploads

This has become one of the biggest changes in the way I think about content.

Instead of saying:

“I made one video.”

I now try to think:

“I created one project. How many useful pieces of content can come from it?”

One MAY production can potentially become:

MAIN CONTENT
cinematic MAY Short;
long-form YouTube breakdown.
EDUCATIONAL SHORTS
character consistency;
camera prompting;
physics prompting;
continuity;
prompt structure.
ENTERTAINMENT CONTENT
failed generations;
unexpected AI mistakes;
viewer reactions;
before-and-after comparisons.
TRAFFIC CONTENT
teaser for the full breakdown;
cliffhanger;
strongest moment from the long-form video;
“How I made this” clip.

Now a single project does not create one upload.

It creates an entire library of content.




That becomes especially valuable in AI filmmaking because generating high-quality footage can be expensive.




The more value you can extract from the footage you already created, the more sustainable your workflow becomes.

Short-form is the top of the funnel

This is where Shorts become much more than a way to collect views.

They become discovery tools.

Someone who has never heard of you can suddenly see one of your videos in their feed.

They watch a MAY clip.

A day later, another one appears.

They recognize the character.

Then maybe they see a behind-the-scenes clip.

They visit your profile.

They discover your full YouTube video.

They watch for six minutes instead of fifteen seconds.

Then they subscribe.

That is powerful.

Short-form creates the first interaction.

Long-form creates a deeper relationship.

You can think of it very simply:

SHORTS = REACH
LONG-FORM = DEPTH
BOTH TOGETHER = CHANNEL GROWTH

And that is increasingly how I want to build my own content.

Do not give everything away in the Short

One mistake I often see is creators trying to fit an entire tutorial into 20 seconds.

Sometimes that works.




But you do not always need to answer every question.

Sometimes the Short should create a new question.

For example:

“Why would I intentionally leave an AI mistake inside a video with millions of views?”

That creates curiosity.

You can give part of the answer.

Then tell people where they can find the complete explanation.




“Full breakdown on YouTube — link in bio.”




The Short creates the question.

The long-form video provides the answer.

That is much more powerful than treating the two formats as completely separate platforms.

Engagement is much bigger than likes

Creators often judge a Short by one number:

Likes.




But engagement goes much further than that.

Someone might:




replay your video;
pause on a particular frame;
open the comments;
write a comment;
reply to someone else;
share the video;
save it;
visit your profile;
watch Part 2;
click through to YouTube;
subscribe;
watch another video.

Those actions tell you far more than a like alone.

And sometimes an imperfection creates exactly that extra interaction.

A viewer thinks:




“Wait… what happened there?”




They replay it.

They inspect it.

They comment.




Suddenly they have spent far longer with your content than the original 15-second runtime.

Do not optimize away your personality

There is another danger in constantly chasing technical perfection.

Your content can start becoming too safe.

Too polished.

Too predictable.




Especially in AI filmmaking, where almost anyone can generate something visually impressive.




The question increasingly becomes:

Why should someone follow you specifically?

Your characters.

Your ideas.

Your humor.

Your filmmaking style.

Your experiments.

Your failures.

Your opinions.




Those are the things that make a creator recognizable.

Not simply perfect pixels.




I would rather post an exciting Short that has one tiny imperfection than a technically flawless piece of content that gives the viewer absolutely no reason to react.

My current short-form strategy

My current strategy is relatively simple.

I aim for around 2 to 4 Shorts per day.

But those Shorts do not all require the same amount of production.

Some are:

HERO CONTENT

The strongest cinematic videos I create.

EXPERIMENTS

New characters, tools, styles or concepts.

LONG-FORM CLIPS

Short moments extracted from longer YouTube videos.

EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

Prompting, camera direction, workflow and filmmaking tips.

BEHIND THE SCENES

How something was made.

ENGAGEMENT CONTENT

Interesting mistakes, reactions, unexpected generations or controversial details.

TRAFFIC CONTENT

Clips specifically designed to send people toward a full YouTube video.

That mix makes consistency much more achievable.

If every Short required maximum effort, publishing 2–4 times per day would become almost impossible.

If every Short required zero effort, quality would disappear.

The goal is finding the balance.

Match the effort to the purpose

This might be the biggest lesson I have learned.

Not:

“Quality doesn't matter.”




And not simply:

“Post as much as possible.”




Instead:

Match the amount of effort to the purpose of the content.

A major cinematic project deserves serious attention.

A commercial deserves precision.

A client deserves polish.

A portfolio film deserves refinement.




But a 15-second Short designed to test whether people care about an idea may not need three days of additional work.




Publish it.

Learn from it.

See what people respond to.

Take the strongest ideas further.

Turn them into long-form.

Then turn that long-form content back into more Shorts.

The result

In just 28 days, this consistent approach helped me reach:

YouTube

~10,000 subscribers
~5,000,000 views

Instagram

~11,000 followers
~10,000,000 views




And those numbers were not created by waiting until every upload was perfect.

They came from:




creating;

publishing;

testing;

learning;

improving;

and doing it again.




Some videos worked.

Some did not.




Some contained imperfections.

Some completely surprised me.




But every upload created another opportunity to reach someone new.

Final thought

Shorts are not just smaller videos.

They are opportunities.

Opportunities to test an idea.

To learn from an audience.

To increase engagement.

To introduce a character.

To build recognition.

To reach millions of people who have never seen your work.

And perhaps most importantly:

to bring someone from a 15-second video into a much bigger story.

That is where I believe the real power of short-form content starts.
