What does it take for an AI ad to feel like a real short film? I make a commercial like this in three steps: assets, scenes and editing. This post follows them in order and also includes the scenes I skip in the video. At the end, I break down what the whole ad cost.

Before You Start

Build every character, outfit, prop and location as an Element before you write any scene. Use the same @ names in Claude and in Higgsfield: when you paste Claude's prompt into Cinema Studio, the matching Elements attach themselves.

I keep everything in one Cinema Studio project, with a folder for each hero.

The Skills

Two skill files are below as .md: Directing Workflow and Dance Direction.

Directing Workflow: turns a short scene request and your references into a shot plan and a full video prompt, with the shots, camera moves and acting.

Dance Direction: turns a dance clip or a written choreography brief into real steps (support foot, weight shifts, arms) inside the video prompt, so you can ask for specific moves instead of "make her dance".

The Plan: Four Beats for Three Heroes

This ad jumps between three heroes, so I build it on a four-beat script: setup, problem, choice, resolution. Every scene I generate gets one of those jobs. That decides how I split each story into blocks and, later, how I cut them together.

Take the goalkeeper: his fall in training is the problem, and the save in the final match is the resolution, so they're two separate blocks. In the edit, the save sits right next to the other two heroes' resolutions.

Step Two — Direct the Scenes

I write the scene prompts with my Directing Workflow skill from the download at the top. It turns a short scene description into a shot plan: shots, camera moves, timing and acting. Load it once in Claude (Customize → Skills → upload the file → switch it on), then mention it in your request.

I split every story into blocks. One block is one prompt, and one prompt can hold several scenes. I run each prompt a few times and cut the best moments from different takes together. Every hero's story follows four beats: setup, problem, choice and resolution.

The Goalkeeper

An injured goalkeeper has one last big match and a knee that might not hold. His story takes five blocks. The clinic, his home and the street are the setup. Training is the problem. The tunnel and the changing room are his choice. The match is the resolution.

Setup — The Clinic and His Home

What it does: introduces him and his injury with two scenes in one prompt. At the clinic the doctor explains the damage, and at home he pulls the knee brace on.

Attach: @loc_doctor, @loc_home, @char_goalkeeper_doctor (his everyday clothes), @char_goalkeeper_home and @prop_bandage (the knee brace).

Tip: if a pose matters, open with a static wide shot. Then the model sees him standing at the table before the camera moves in. If the first frame is too close, the model has to guess the pose.

Step Three — Editing

What it does: cuts the three stories into one film, switching heroes when they hit the same beat.

Make each story work on its own first. Intercutting won't save a weak story; it only hides the flaws for a few seconds. Then give every cut a clear reason: matching beats.

Quality and Cost

This project cost up to about 8,500 credits, counting the takes I didn't use: about 100 for preparation, 3,900 for the goalkeeper, 2,500 for the dancer and 2,000 for the schoolboy. A real shoot with three casts, a stadium and a dance battle would cost hundreds of thousands. If you make a commercial like this for a brand, put the credits into the production budget.

The whole project took 72 hours. What I'd take into the next one: approach the story as a director. Before you generate anything, know how that shot makes the story work.
