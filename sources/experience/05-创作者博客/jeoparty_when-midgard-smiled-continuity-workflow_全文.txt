A practical continuity workflow for creating one believable Nordic world with recurring characters, connected shots and Seedance 2.5.

WHEN MIDGARD SMILED did not begin as a request for “a cinematic Viking video.” It began with a world, an original song, recurring characters and one harder question:

How do you make dozens of AI-generated shots feel as if they belong to the same story?

The finished Higgsfield Open Project contains 33 generations: three world-building images, 29 Seedance 2.5 story clips and one final 1080p upscale of the completed 47-second teaser.

Watch and explore the full Open Project here. The prompts are visible, so you can inspect the generations behind the final result.

1. Begin with story function, not spectacle

The project belongs to VølvaEchoes, a connected storyworld built through original music, Nordic myth and recurring characters. That meant every shot needed a reason to exist beyond looking cinematic.

Before generating, I reduced the teaser to a simple dramatic movement:

Establish the mythic world.
Introduce the Vølva and the raven.
Use the raven's movement to carry us into Midgard.
Find the children and follow their journey.
Let small acts of community become the emotional payoff.
End inside a shared hall rather than on another epic landscape.

That last choice mattered. The teaser's goal was not to prove that the world was large. It was to make the world feel inhabited.

For every planned clip, I asked: What changes during this shot? If the answer was only “the camera moves,” the shot was not ready.

2. Build a visual bible before animating the sequence

The first three generations were not movie shots. They were reference images:

A daytime Midgard panorama
A night version of the same settlement
A coastal cliff and Vølva viewpoint

These established the project's visual boundaries: climate, settlement density, materials, coastline, architecture, lighting logic and scale.

A useful visual bible does not need dozens of images. It needs a few images that repeatedly answer the same questions: What materials dominate the world? How weathered is everything? What kind of light belongs here? How large are the buildings relative to the characters? Which details must never drift into another era or genre?

Without those anchors, every new prompt quietly redesigns the film.

3. Create a continuity chain, not 29 isolated clips

The Seedance 2.5 clips were treated as links in one chain. Each accepted generation inherited a limited set of responsibilities from the previous shot: character count, direction of movement, geography, time of day, emotional tone and the next story action.

The sequence moved from the Vølva on the cliff, through the raven's flight, down toward the settlement, across the children's journey, into the market and workshops, and finally toward the communal hall.

One shot establishes.
The next shot continues.
A bridge shot solves a transition.
A reaction shot protects the emotional rhythm.
A rejected shot never enters the continuity chain.

That final rule is important. A beautiful generation can still be wrong for the film.

4. Separate the prompt into responsibilities

Long prompts are not automatically precise prompts. I found it more useful to build them in layers:

Continuity: who is present, where they came from and what must remain unchanged.
Action: one primary action that can clearly finish during the clip.
Camera: viewpoint, movement, pace and framing—but only when it supports the action.
World: materials, weather, light, environment and period cues.
End-state: the exact frame condition needed by the next shot.
Exclusions: the most likely continuity failures to prevent.

Here are three short excerpts from the actual project prompts:

“Preserve the exact same Vølva: identical adult face, freckles and skin texture, dark eye makeup and fine vertical facial markings...”

“The first output frame is the immediate next frame after the seed's final frame. Do not replay or regenerate the doorway passage.”

“No visible raven, no beak, no wings, no feathers... Do not add, remove, duplicate or relocate islands, buildings, roads, boats, mountains or shoreline.”

The point is not to make every prompt equally long. The point is to name the responsibility of every sentence. If a shot failed because of identity drift, I could strengthen the continuity layer without rewriting the whole idea.

5. Selection mattered more than generation count

Several story beats needed multiple versions. The winning version was not always the most dramatic one.

I rejected or replaced clips when they caused a recurring character to change identity, altered the number of children, reversed movement without motivation, made the geography impossible, skipped an essential story beat or became more spectacular but less believable.

A practical review method is to score each result in four categories:

Story: does the intended beat happen?
Continuity: does it connect to the shots around it?
Readability: can the viewer understand it immediately?
Beauty: is it visually strong?

Beauty matters—but it comes fourth. A sequence made entirely from individually beautiful shots can still feel like a montage of unrelated films.

6. Let the music shape the visual arc

VølvaEchoes begins with original music, so the soundtrack was not decoration added at the end. Its layers helped decide where the visual world should open, accelerate, soften and arrive.

The raven's flight gave the teaser forward movement. The children and settlement introduced human scale. The communal ending gave the music somewhere emotionally meaningful to land.

This is one of the biggest opportunities in AI filmmaking: music and visuals do not have to be separate products. They can be designed as two expressions of the same storyworld.

7. Finish the storytelling before the upscale

The final project asset is the assembled teaser upscaled to 1080p. Upscaling came after the story, continuity and pacing decisions were resolved.

Resolution cannot repair a weak transition, a confused action or a character who changes between shots. Use the upscale as a delivery step, not a rescue operation.

8. What did not work—and what it cost

The hardest failures were predictable: crowded scenes increased identity drift, vague transitions produced location jumps, and ambitious multi-action prompts made the model choose which instruction to ignore.

The public project page reports 852 credits spent across the full build. I do not present that as a recommended budget. I see it as evidence that uncontrolled iteration becomes expensive.

The better future workflow is not “generate less at all costs.” It is:

Define the story beat more clearly.
Generate with one explicit failure test.
Stop when the shot performs its function.
Keep alternates only when they solve a real editing problem.
9. What I would lock earlier next time
Character reference sheets for every recurring child
A simple map of the settlement and travel direction
A shot ledger recording the accepted version of every scene
Transition plans before generating the surrounding shots
A smaller set of reusable continuity phrases
Clear stop conditions for each generation

The goal is not to remove discovery. It is to give discovery a structure.

Reusable checklist

Before generating

What changes in this shot?
Which previous shot must it connect to?
Which character and world details are non-negotiable?
What is the single biggest failure risk?

Before accepting

Is the story beat readable without explanation?
Do identity, direction, geography and light still match?
Does this improve the sequence—not just the asset folder?
Would I keep it if it were slightly less beautiful?

Before finishing

Do the music and image progression share the same arc?
Are any transitions solved only by wishful thinking?
Is the final upscale improving delivery rather than hiding problems?
What comes next

WHEN MIDGARD SMILED is the first public window into a larger VølvaEchoes storyworld. The next films will carry forward what this one taught me: AI continuity becomes much more manageable when every generation inherits a clear narrative responsibility.
