How I Made The Perfect One: Bringing a Psychological Horror Story to Life with Higgsfield

By Sharif Elfishawi, creator of The Perfect One and founder of WAMBOH

Two years before making The Perfect One, I had already written the story I wanted to turn into a feature film. Bringing it to the screen required resources I didn’t yet have. The screenplay existed, but I needed a way to let people experience its atmosphere, its characters, and the unsettling idea at its center.

Higgsfield gave me a way to build that first step: a three-minute psychological horror short and proof of concept for the Higgsfield Global Film Festival.

The premise comes down to one sentence:

They didn’t want a child. They wanted the perfect one.

That idea guided the production, from the family’s appearance to the way their affection gradually becomes uncomfortable.

Finding the right piece of the story

For the short, I focused on a prequel within the feature’s universe. I wanted to introduce the family and suggest a history the audience could feel without fully understanding.

The biggest creative decision was how much to withhold. In three minutes, every explanation takes time away from an experience. I wanted viewers to notice a look, a reaction, or an ordinary object and start asking questions.

The opening needed warmth. If the parents seemed threatening immediately, the audience would have no reason to trust them. Their affection had to feel believable for its intensity to become disturbing later.

That became the emotional progression: a beautiful family memory, a small feeling that something is wrong, and a growing sense of confinement.

Building characters and locations before scenes




A film needs the audience to recognize the same people across different shots, lighting conditions, and moments in time.

I built the workflow around reusable character, location, and prop references. The family appears across different periods, which meant establishing younger and older versions of the parents while preserving their identity.

The environments needed the same attention. The mountain house, its rooms, and the surrounding landscape had to belong to one recognizable world.

I also created references for objects that mattered to the story. One example is an old handheld digital pet. Its size, appearance, and the way it passes between characters all affect whether the scene feels believable.

Those details become especially important when an object carries a mystery. The audience needs to follow what is happening without being distracted by something changing between shots.

Turning each scene into specific instructions




When developing scenes in Higgsfield, I worked through the blocking and continuity as carefully as the visual description.

Who is sitting where? Who is holding the object? What can the camera see? What should remain outside the frame? What changes emotionally during the shot?

The dinner sequence made those questions particularly important. It brings together recurring characters, fixed seating, dialogue, and a small prop that triggers a reaction. The scene depends on the relationship between those details.

I used image references to help establish composition and scale, then described the action around them. Giving each scene a clear dramatic purpose helped keep the generations connected to the larger story.

Keeping the horror inside ordinary behavior




I wanted the film’s unease to grow through the family dynamic.

A parent looking at a child can be tender. Hold that attention a little too intensely, and its meaning begins to change. A protective reaction can feel reassuring until it becomes disproportionate.

That balance shaped the visual direction. The early family imagery needed room to feel nostalgic and affectionate. Later, the isolated house, darkness, and storm could increase the pressure as the audience’s understanding changed.

I was careful about what the frame revealed. Keeping something unseen can give a small reaction more weight than an explicit image would.

The challenge was maintaining that restraint across the sequence, so the darker moments felt earned.







Giving the sound the same emotional journey

My background in music made the soundtrack an important part of developing the film’s identity.

The musical brief followed the same progression as the images: begin warm, beautiful, and slightly strange, then gradually let that musical world become more obsessive and unsettling.

I developed the soundtrack direction around a continuous Suno composition. The intention was for a recognizable musical idea to change meaning as the family’s behavior changed meaning.

Rain, thunder, room ambience, and pauses around dialogue also mattered. The sound needed to leave space for the audience to register a reaction or a line before moving forward.




What I wanted the proof of concept to achieve

The Perfect One gave me a way to show the tone and visual identity of a story that had previously lived on the page.

The ambition remains to develop the feature with real actors and a real crew. Having a short within that universe makes the conversation more tangible: people can experience the atmosphere and understand the kind of film I want to make.

That is the opportunity I see in tools like Higgsfield. A writer can take a project that has been waiting for resources and create something concrete enough to share.

For me, the most valuable part was seeing the story begin to exist outside my own imagination and giving it a chance to reach the people who could help bring the feature to life.
