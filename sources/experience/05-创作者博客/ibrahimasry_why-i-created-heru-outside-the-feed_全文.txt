The Dead Are Still Online

Imagine finding someone you lost.

They recognize you. They remember your life together. Their voice is familiar enough to make you forget, for a moment, that they are gone.

Then you have to ask whether anyone is actually there.

That uncertainty sits at the heart of HERU — OUTSIDE THE FEED.

Horus travels across time as Heru, searching for his lost love. He arrives in a transformed Alexandria, where intelligent boxes think for the living and digital models speak for the dead. The world offers him something almost impossible to refuse: another conversation with her.

I wanted that hope to pull him forward. When you desperately want someone back, even a familiar voice can feel like a doorway.

The world around him has made staying connected effortless. Boxes exchange messages, make decisions, and maintain relationships on behalf of their owners. Two digital identities can keep a conversation going while the people they represent take no part in it.

A relationship can remain active in the network long after the people inside it have stopped showing up.

That is why I put boxes where faces should be. A face lets us notice hesitation, embarrassment, affection, or the effort of finding the right words. In Heru’s world, a system takes that place. It can answer for you before you have worked out what you feel.

The connection count goes up. The distance between people can keep growing.

Bringing an ancient Egyptian identity into this world gave the story another tension. Heru recognizes the desire to preserve someone. He comes from a culture whose relationship with death left bodies, monuments, and images that survived for thousands of years.

Now preservation answers back.

A model can carry a familiar face, recall a shared memory, and continue speaking in someone’s name. For Heru, the question becomes painfully personal: what part of the woman he loves has survived?

Alexandria keeps that question close to everyday life. Old streets, pavement cafés, museum spaces, and the sea surround a world increasingly lived through digital substitutes. I wanted the physical city to remain present: a place where people could still encounter each other, if they chose to.

Making this film with AI placed me inside the question too. I was generating faces and performances while telling a story about the uncertainty of a generated presence. It made me pay attention to the pauses: how long Heru looks, when he hesitates, and what a moment leaves unresolved.

His search eventually turns toward himself. The same world promising to preserve his lost love also offers to preserve him. Being recognized, remembered, and permanently available has its own pull.

I wanted the audience to feel that temptation with him.

If you are making films here on Higgsfield, I would love to hear how you approached presence in your own work. What made a generated character feel emotionally real to you?

And after watching HERU, I’m curious:

If someone you loved could keep answering you, what would convince you they were still there?
