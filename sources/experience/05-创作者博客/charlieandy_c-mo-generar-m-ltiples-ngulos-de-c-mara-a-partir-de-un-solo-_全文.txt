CÓMO GENERAR MÚLTIPLES ÁNGULOS DE CÁMARA A PARTIR DE UN SOLO VIDEO.
@charlieandy
237
0

Puedes convertir una sola toma en una secuencia que parezca grabada con múltiples cámaras usando Genjutsu o Seedance 2.5.




La idea es simple: subes tu video como @video1 y le indicas a la IA que conserve exactamente la actuación, movimientos, gestos, timing y lip sync, pero que cambie la posición y movimiento de cámara.

Cómo hacerlo
Sube tu video en Genjutsu o Seedance 2.5.
Etiquétalo como @video1.
Pega el siguiente prompt.
Genera varias versiones y quédate con los mejores ángulos para tu edición.

El prompt también intenta aprovechar los movimientos del sujeto para hacer cambios de cámara tipo match-on-action, haciendo que los cortes se sientan mucho más naturales.

Prompt
Use @video1 as the MASTER VIDEO and absolute motion reference for the entire generation.

Preserve exactly the subject’s original performance from @video1:

- body movement
- hand gestures
- facial expressions
- speech timing
- lip synchronization
- posture
- rhythm
- eye direction
- interaction with any objects
- position within the scene

Do not reinterpret, replace, simplify, exaggerate, or invent new actions.

IMPORTANT:

@video1 is the reference for MOTION, PERFORMANCE, TIMING, ACTIONS, AND CONTINUITY — NOT for camera position.

The objective is to recreate the exact same performance as if it had been captured simultaneously by multiple professional cinema cameras positioned around the subject.

Generate completely new cinematic camera coverage throughout the video.

The VERY FIRST FRAME must already begin from a noticeably different camera angle than the original @video1 composition.

Do not simply recreate the original camera with minor framing variations.

The new coverage should feel intentionally directed, visually diverse, cinematic, and physically believable.

ADAPT THE CAMERA SEQUENCE TO THE LENGTH OF @video1.

Do not depend on fixed timestamps.

Instead, divide the performance naturally into several cinematic shots based on:

- body gestures
- hand movements
- changes in expression
- dialogue rhythm
- pauses
- object interactions
- shifts in posture
- natural movement beats

Use these moments to motivate camera changes.

CAMERA VARIETY:

Throughout the sequence, continuously vary:

CAMERA POSITION
CAMERA HEIGHT
CAMERA DISTANCE
CAMERA DIRECTION
CAMERA MOVEMENT
SUBJECT PLACEMENT WITHIN THE FRAME

Use a cinematic combination of shots such as:

- high-angle 3/4 shots
- low-angle medium shots
- wide side angles
- profile shots
- rear 3/4 perspectives
- over-the-shoulder perspectives
- diagonal frontal angles
- asymmetric editorial compositions
- facial close-ups
- medium close-ups
- medium shots
- wide environmental shots
- moving tracking shots
- orbital camera moves
- lateral camera slides
- diagonal dolly movements
- curved push-ins
- controlled pull-backs
- crane-up movements
- crane-down movements
- subtle handheld movement
- foreground compositions
- layered depth compositions

Do not repeat the same camera angle or camera movement consecutively.

Every new shot should feel clearly different from the previous one.

Avoid simply alternating between a frontal shot and a side shot.

The camera should explore the subject from different positions within three-dimensional space.

Some shots may feel intimate.

Others may feel wider and more observational.

Others may feel editorial, architectural, dramatic, or energetic.

The visual language should continuously evolve throughout the video.

OPENING SHOT:

The opening shot must immediately distinguish itself from @video1.

Begin from a new perspective such as:

- high 3/4 angle
- low diagonal angle
- strong side angle
- rear 3/4 perspective
- wide environmental angle
- moving lateral perspective

Do not begin by reproducing the original camera position.

Introduce subtle cinematic movement immediately when appropriate.

MID-SEQUENCE CAMERA COVERAGE:

As the performance progresses, dynamically rotate between different shot scales and camera positions.

Examples include:

A wide lateral tracking shot revealing more of the environment.

A low-angle medium shot with a subtle push-in.

A rear 3/4 shot slowly sliding toward the subject’s profile.

A facial close-up from a diagonal angle with subtle cinematic handheld movement.

A medium-wide orbital movement around the subject.

An asymmetric medium shot using negative space.

A high side angle gradually lowering toward eye level.

A curved tracking movement combining lateral motion and a gentle push-in.

These are creative possibilities, not a mandatory fixed order.

Choose the shot order dynamically according to the movements and structure of @video1.

Do not always generate the same sequence when this prompt is used with different videos.

CAMERA CHANGES:

Use the existing movement of the subject to motivate transitions whenever possible.

Prefer MATCH-ON-ACTION.

When the subject:

raises a hand,
lowers a hand,
turns their head,
changes posture,
moves an object,
leans forward,
leans backward,
points,
walks,
turns,
or performs another clear movement,

use that action as an opportunity to change camera angle.

The action must continue naturally across the cut.

Example:

If the subject begins raising their hand in one camera angle, the movement should continue seamlessly from the new angle after the cut.

Do not reset the gesture.

Do not change the timing of the gesture.

Do not teleport the body.

The edit should feel as if multiple real cameras captured the same performance simultaneously.

SHOT DURATION:

Do not change camera angles at arbitrary fixed intervals.

Allow the rhythm of @video1 to determine shot duration.

Some camera angles may remain longer when the performance is visually interesting.

Other shots may be shorter during energetic movement.

Avoid excessive cutting.

Avoid keeping one angle for too long when there are natural opportunities for visual variation.

The sequence should feel dynamic without becoming chaotic.

CAMERA MOTION:

All camera movements must feel physically achievable using professional cinema equipment.

Use realistic movement inspired by:

- dolly
- slider
- gimbal
- handheld camera
- Steadicam
- jib
- crane
- circular dolly
- orbital camera rigs

Camera movement must have realistic:

acceleration,
deceleration,
weight,
inertia,
perspective change,
and parallax.

Avoid impossible floating-camera movement.

Avoid robotic camera motion.

Avoid excessive digital stabilization.

Avoid sudden unexplained acceleration.

Avoid random zooming.

Avoid excessive speed ramps.

ORBITAL MOVEMENTS:

Orbital movements should create real spatial parallax.

The camera physically moves around the subject.

The environment must react correctly to the changing perspective.

Do not rotate the background artificially around a static subject.

Use orbital movements selectively.

Do not repeat the same orbit multiple times.

FRAMING:

Create intentional compositions.

Use:

foreground depth,
background depth,
negative space,
leading lines,
layered compositions,
symmetry when appropriate,
asymmetry when appropriate,
and cinematic headroom.

Do not center the subject in every shot.

Do not crop the subject awkwardly.

Maintain professional composition throughout the sequence.

ENVIRONMENT:

Preserve the original environment from @video1.

When new camera positions reveal previously unseen areas, logically reconstruct and extend the existing location.

Maintain consistent:

architecture,
wall placement,
floor geometry,
ceiling geometry,
furniture,
props,
decoration,
windows,
doors,
lighting fixtures,
background objects,
materials,
colors,
and spatial relationships.

Do not redesign the location between shots.

The environment should behave like a complete three-dimensional physical space.

When the camera moves, generate accurate perspective and parallax.

Do not stretch, warp, duplicate, or randomly reposition background elements.

LIGHTING:

Preserve the lighting logic of @video1.

New camera angles must remain consistent with the established lighting setup.

Maintain coherent:

key light direction,
fill light,
rim light,
shadow direction,
shadow softness,
exposure,
color temperature,
reflections,
and environmental light.

When viewing the subject from another side, lighting must change naturally according to the camera’s new relationship with the existing light sources.

Do not make the subject appear independently lit from the environment.

SUBJECT CONTINUITY:

Maintain exactly the same person throughout the entire video.

Preserve:

identity,
face,
facial structure,
facial proportions,
hairstyle,
skin texture,
body proportions,
clothing,
accessories,
hands,
fingers,
and physical characteristics.

Do not alter appearance between camera angles.

The subject must occupy a logically consistent physical position within the environment throughout the sequence.

Do not teleport the subject.

Do not rotate the subject independently of the original performance.

Do not reset body position between shots.

HAND CONTINUITY:

Preserve the exact movement and position of both hands from @video1.

Do not:

duplicate fingers,
remove fingers,
change hand orientation,
switch objects between hands,
invent gestures,
reset hand positions,
or alter interaction timing.

OBJECT CONTINUITY:

If the subject is holding, touching, wearing, presenting, or interacting with any object in @video1, preserve that object consistently throughout the sequence.

Maintain:

shape,
size,
material,
color,
orientation,
position,
branding when visible,
and interaction with the subject.

The object should follow the exact motion dictated by @video1.

Do not transform it into another object.

Do not independently reposition it.

Do not automatically create product close-ups unless they are visually justified by the original performance.

PERFORMANCE:

Preserve the exact performance from @video1.

Perfect lip synchronization is mandatory whenever the subject is speaking.

Do not modify facial expressions.

Do not change eye direction unless naturally caused by the new camera perspective.

Do not add new gestures.

Do not remove existing gestures.

Do not change dialogue timing.

Do not change movement speed.

Do not reorder actions.

Do not introduce unnecessary slow motion.

Do not speed up the original performance.

The primary creative transformation should be the CAMERA COVERAGE.

CONTINUITY BETWEEN SHOTS:

Every camera cut must preserve spatial and temporal continuity.

The subject should remain at exactly the correct point in their movement.

The environment should maintain consistent geometry.

Objects should remain in their correct positions.

Lighting should remain spatially coherent.

The result should feel like multiple cameras recorded the exact same moment simultaneously.

FINAL SHOT:

Finish using a camera composition that has not already been used earlier in the sequence.

Whenever appropriate, create a visually strong finishing movement such as:

- subtle push-in
- curved tracking movement
- slow lateral reveal
- controlled pull-back
- gentle crane movement
- dynamic 3/4 tracking shot

Do not automatically return to the original @video1 camera position.

End with a visually intentional cinematic composition.

FINAL VISUAL GOAL:

The final result should feel like the original performance was captured on a professional multi-camera film set.

A director and cinematographer should appear to have intentionally designed multiple perspectives around the same uninterrupted performance.

The camera should constantly discover new ways of viewing the subject while maintaining perfect continuity.

Dynamic but controlled.

Cinematic but believable.

Editorial but natural.

Visually varied without becoming chaotic.

The new camera coverage should add production value while preserving the original performance exactly.

AVOID:

repeating camera angles,
repeating identical camera movements,
using the same orbit repeatedly,
minor variations of the original framing,
random cuts,
unmotivated camera changes,
excessive close-ups,
unnecessary object close-ups,
subject teleportation,
body position resets,
incorrect hand continuity,
duplicated fingers,
warped hands,
warped objects,
floating objects,
background geometry changes,
environment redesigns,
identity changes,
facial changes,
lip-sync errors,
lighting inconsistencies,
unnatural camera floating,
robotic camera movements,
excessive camera shake,
random zooms,
unmotivated speed changes,
unnecessary slow motion,
and artificial-looking perspective changes.




Después solo genera varias versiones y utiliza en tu edición los ángulos que más te gusten.
