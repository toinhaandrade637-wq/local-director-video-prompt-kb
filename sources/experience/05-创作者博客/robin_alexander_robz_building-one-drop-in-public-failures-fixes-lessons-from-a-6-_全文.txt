Behind the scenes of ONE DROP — from concept to final film.




ONE DROP started with one question:


What happens when something as basic as water is no longer a human right, but something controlled by a system?

That question slowly became a world — a future where water is currency, machines enforce the rules, and a robot designed without emotion begins to understand something its creators never intended it to learn.

Making this film became much more than generating a series of AI shots. I had to develop the story, build the world, create and lock characters, maintain continuity across scenes, direct performances, experiment with different AI models, edit, add sound and dialogue, and sometimes throw away generations that simply didn’t work.

I also decided to build ONE DROP in public. That changed the process completely. People started watching while the film was still evolving, leaving feedback, questioning decisions, supporting the project — and sometimes pointing out weaknesses I couldn’t see after working on the film for so long.

This is a look behind that process: what worked, what failed, and what I learned while turning ONE DROP from an idea into a six-minute sci-fi film.




Building a World Before Building the Shots




Before generating the film, I needed ONE DROP to exist as a consistent world.

Characters, robots, locations, vehicles, props and even the water system had to follow the same visual language from one scene to the next. I learned quickly that a good individual generation means very little if the next shot looks like it belongs to a different film.

So I started treating every important element as a production asset rather than simply prompting each scene from scratch.

More in Elements.




Each important character and object was created as a reusable Element. Instead of redesigning them for every shot, I could reference the same visual identity throughout production.

This helped me keep the boy, dog, UNIT7, SECBOT and the rest of the world recognizable across different locations, lighting conditions, camera angles and scenes.

It wasn’t perfect — consistency remained one of the hardest parts of the film — but building the Elements first gave me a visual foundation to work from.




From Elements to Scenes




Creating a character was only the beginning.

The real challenge was placing those characters into cinematic scenes while preserving their identity, scale, wardrobe, environment and relationship to each other.

A shot could look beautiful and still be unusable if one character changed, an important prop disappeared, or the geography no longer matched the previous scene. I ended up regenerating many shots not because they looked bad, but because they didn’t belong to the same film.




When AI Gets It Wrong




A Lesson That Saved Me Credits

One thing I learned after many failed generations: the more logically you direct a scene, the less you depend on luck.

Before generating, I now think about where every character starts, what they need to do, where the camera is positioned, how the camera should move, and what the final frame needs to achieve.

I also found that my safest playground is roughly 5–15 seconds per generation. If a scene needs more time, I prefer breaking it into multiple purposeful shots rather than asking the model to perform too many actions in one generation.

Shorter doesn’t automatically mean better. A simple continuous action can work beautifully. The problems usually begin when one generation is expected to handle too many character movements, camera changes, interactions and story beats at once.




My rule became simple: direct the scene first, generate second.

That reduced unnecessary retries, improved continuity, and most importantly, saved credits.




When It Finally Works




After all the failed generations, there are moments when everything finally comes together — the character stays consistent, the movement feels natural, the camera does what you intended, and the shot actually carries the emotion of the scene.

Those moments made all the retries worth it.

For me, the biggest improvement wasn’t learning how to write longer prompts. It was learning how to simplify the intention of each shot. One shot doesn’t need to explain the whole scene. It just needs to do its job well.




Building in Public Changed the Film




Publishing ONE DROP while I was still developing it gave me something I normally wouldn’t have during production: real audience feedback.

Some reactions confirmed what was working. Others made me reconsider pacing, clarity and certain creative decisions. I went back into the edit, shortened sections, regenerated shots and experimented with different ways of telling the same moment.

I didn’t follow every suggestion. That wasn’t the point. The value was being able to see the film through fresh eyes while I still had time to improve it.

The audience didn’t make the film for me — they helped me see the film differently.
