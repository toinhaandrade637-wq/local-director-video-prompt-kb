The brief

I once asked Echoes of Embers if he was up for a collab, the answer is this video/blog.

My job was to give it a body. Not a lyric video, and not a wallpaper loop. A scene from a film that was never made: nineteen seconds, one location, one moment. A wounded knight, a tree older than the kingdom he came from, and a city burning violet across the valley. One rule decided everything else: nothing in it may look digital. If a frame could have come out of a render farm, it failed.

Rule one: lock the look before the model sees a word

The look is not a style preset here, it is a sentence I reuse word for word. It sits at the front of every prompt, and the only part that changes is the description in the middle of it.


cinematic film still from a 1980s dark fantasy movie, [description], shot on 35mm film, grainy, cozy, in the style of the neverending story in 1984, 35mm film grain.



Two words in there are load-bearing. "Grainy" keeps the film stock alive in the shadows, and "cozy" is not decoration. It is the thing that keeps a dark scene from becoming a grim one. A 1980s fantasy frame frightens you and shelters you at the same time. Remove "cozy" and the same composition turns into a game screenshot.

The location first, with nobody in it

I build the empty world in Midjourney (but you can do it before I put anyone in it. If you ask for a figure and a landscape in the same breath, the model spends its attention on the figure and the place comes back small and generic. Scale is what you are actually buying, and scale comes from architecture: countless towers, stairways that take ten seconds to walk, banners the size of a bed sheet.

Cinematic 1980s dark fantasy film still. Abandoned royal garden on a mountain precipice: a vast carpet of small ivory flowers between weathered flagstones, a low circular fountain with a dry cracked basin in the foreground, its stone rim surrounded by flowers. Broken gothic balustrades overlook an enormous valley with a distant medieval kingdom of slender towers descending into mist, a colossal ruined cathedral rising above. Last amber sunlight breaks beneath a deep violet storm front, illuminating distant spires while the garden falls into cool blue shadow. Scattered pale petals on stone. Wide panoramic composition conveying immense scale. Deserted, no people or animals. Shot on 35mm, vintage practical miniatures and matte painted backgrounds, style of The NeverEnding Story (1984), anamorphic lens, Kodak film grain, gentle halation, slight optical softness.


The trick in that last sentence is temperature. Warm light on the heights, cold blue in the depths. It gives a flat image a top and a bottom, which is most of what "depth" means in a still.

The knight

He never shows a face. Not because the model cannot make one, but because a closed visor turns him into a shape you can place yourself inside. Identity comes from posture and from the cape instead. One figure, one light direction.

Full body depiction of a weary medieval knight on a seamless neutral grey background, facing forward, entire body visible head to toe. Fully enclosed silver steel helmet, closed visor, black horizontal eye slit, central ridge, reinforced bevor. Face entirely concealed. Scarred fluted silver plate armor with darkened patina, rounded pauldrons, articulated breastplate, steel gauntlets, overlapping thigh plates, greaves, armored boots. Dark chainmail visible at neck, elbows, hips. Faded burgundy wool cape from bronze shoulder clasps, hem at calves. Narrow brown leather belt, plain longsword in weathered dark leather scabbard at left hip. Hands at sides, feet slightly apart, neutral stance. Realistic proportions, centered composition, scratched steel, chainmail, worn leather and wool textures, soft balanced studio lighting. Vintage 1981 dark fantasy film still, anamorphic lens, 35mm Kodak grain, low key cinematic lighting with readable shadow detail.

Create a precise three-view character reference sheet of the armored knight in the uploaded image. Use this image as the primary authority for the character’s proportions, silhouette, armor construction, helmet, clothing, equipment, materials and colors. Preserve the actual reference design rather than substituting generic medieval armor.

CHARACTER DESIGN

A tall, fully enclosed knight with realistic human proportions and an intricate, vertically elongated silhouette. All skin, hair, eyes and facial features remain concealed.

The helmet is tall and sharply pointed, with a pronounced central ridge and sculpted, leaf-like fluting. A narrow, downward-angled horizontal visor opening forms a shallow V across the front, completely black inside. The elongated faceplate descends into a flared, pointed lower edge overlapping the upper chest. Preserve this distinctive helmet shape precisely.

The armor is weathered dark silver steel with bright worn edges, fine scratches, mottled patina and deep dark recesses. Its overlapping plates resemble elongated leaves, with raised central ribs and flowing pointed contours. Preserve the sculpted breastplate, large leaf-shaped pauldrons, layered arm defenses, flared gauntlet cuffs, articulated metal fingers, overlapping pointed hip plates, ridged knee defenses, long fluted greaves and pointed articulated sabatons.

Dark chainmail forms a broad mantle around the shoulders and upper chest and remains visible beneath the arm plates, along the sides of the torso and between the leg defenses. Preserve the hanging chainmail sections and their length.

A heavy, faded burgundy cape attaches at both shoulders beneath small ornamental bronze-toned fittings. It falls to the ground in deep folds, with a frayed, ragged hem and slight pooling around the feet. Preserve its volume, fabric weight and muted wine-red color.

A narrow brown leather belt wraps around the waist, with a diagonal leather suspension strap across the hips. A single sword hangs at the knight’s anatomical right hip, appearing on the viewer’s left in the front view. Match the reference’s rounded metal pommel, dark grip, straight crossguard and long weathered brown scabbard. Preserve this anatomical side in every view; do not mirror the equipment.

LAYOUT

Use one horizontal landscape canvas with three equal-width viewing zones, arranged left to right: front body without head, complete rear body, enlarged helmet-and-upper-torso portrait.

Center each view within its own third. Maintain clear background around each silhouette so neighboring views do not overlap. Use one continuous neutral medium-grey studio background and floor across the entire sheet, without borders, divider lines, frames or visible panel seams.

LEFT THIRD: FRONT BODY, HEAD OMITTED

Show the knight directly from the front in a neutral symmetrical stance, arms relaxed at the sides, hands open naturally, feet slightly apart.

First establish complete full-body framing, including the hypothetical helmet position. Then omit only the head and helmet above the neckline, including the helmet’s elongated faceplate. Retain the chainmail collar, shoulder mantle, cape attachments and every independent torso armor component.

Leave plain background where the helmet would have been. This is a clean reference-sheet omission, with no exposed anatomy, wound, stump or detached helmet.

Keep the torso, shoulders, hands, hips, knees and feet at their original full-body positions. Do not move the body upward or enlarge it to occupy the empty head space. Keep both feet and the complete cape hem inside the canvas.

MIDDLE THIRD: COMPLETE REAR VIEW

Show the same knight directly from behind, including the entire helmet, cape and feet, in the matching neutral stance.

Preserve the helmet’s pointed crown and longitudinal ridges as they continue naturally around the rear. Show the heavy burgundy cape hanging naturally from the same shoulder attachments and covering the back. Do not remove, lift, shorten, spread or make the cape transparent to expose hidden armor.

Keep the visible rear and side portions of the armor consistent with the front design. The sword remains at the knight’s anatomical right hip, appearing on the viewer’s right in this rear view, and is only visible where the cape naturally permits.

The reference does not establish the complete rear construction. Resolve unseen areas conservatively through simple, plausible continuations of the existing forms. Add no new emblems, weapons, ornaments or costume elements.

MATCHED BODY SCALE

The front and rear views must use identical subject scale, camera height, viewing distance and restrained perspective. Align the shoulder line, waist, hips, knees and soles of the feet across both views.

Use the same ground baseline and the same hypothetical complete helmet-to-foot height. The front view’s omitted helmet must leave unused space, not trigger a change in framing.

RIGHT THIRD: HELMET AND UPPER-TORSO DETAIL

Show an enlarged portrait from the upper chest upward, including the complete helmet, chainmail collar, both shoulder attachments and enough of the pauldrons to explain their construction.

Turn the knight slightly toward the knight’s anatomical right, with the visor pointing slightly toward the viewer’s left. Keep the front visor and the knight’s left helmet surface visible in a restrained three-quarter view.

Preserve the pointed crown, central ridge, narrow black visor slit, leaf-like fluting and elongated flared faceplate. Keep clear background above the helmet tip and around its silhouette.

The visor remains fully closed and dark. Do not reveal or invent eyes, a face, hair or exposed skin.

LIGHTING AND CONTINUITY

Use soft, balanced studio illumination with consistent direction, exposure and color balance across all views. Reveal the dark steel’s raised ridges, scratches, chainmail links, leather straps and burgundy fabric without blowing out highlights or burying details in shadow.

Preserve the reference’s tactile costume materials and subtle photographic texture. Keep all views sufficiently sharp for design comparison, with minimal perspective distortion and no shallow-focus blur.

All three views depict one identical knight. Maintain matching armor construction, cape color, chainmail arrangement, fittings, proportions and equipment placement.

EXCLUSIONS

No text, labels, diagrams, measurements, logos, watermarks, scenery or decorative graphics. No additional props beyond the sword and scabbard already established in the reference. No additional weapons, shields, crowns, horns, glowing eyes or magical effects. No exposed face, open visor, costume variations, mirrored equipment, missing armor components, cropped feet or cropped helmet tip.
The part that decides the whole piece: the handoff to video

This is where most AI films fall apart. Text-to-video invents its own camera, its own framing and its own rhythm, and it will happily throw away the composition you spent an hour on. So the image carries the composition, and the video prompt only carries time.

Nineteen seconds, seven shots, ordered:

cinematic 1980s dark fantasy film sequence, seven shots in one location and one continuous moment, shot on 35mm film, grainy, in the style of the neverending story in 1984, 35mm film grain.

Shot 1: locked medium shot on the chest and thighs of a knight in polished silver plate armour, torn crimson cape hanging in strips, the plates catching the last low light, his breathing moving the breastplate, no face visible.

Shot 2: locked wide shot from the forest floor, an immense moss-covered tree dark in the left foreground, a deep valley opening beyond it, a fortified city far below glowing violet in the mist.

Shot 3: locked medium shot from behind the knight as he leans his weight against the tree trunk, one arm braced, the torn cape lifting in a slow wind.

Shot 4: locked medium shot as he slides down the trunk and settles at its base, armour creaking, the movement slow and heavy, the camera still.

Shot 5: locked close shot on the helmet and upper chest, visor closed dark, a gauntleted hand pressed flat against a dark stain on the breastplate.

Shot 6: over-the-shoulder shot from behind the knight, his helm dark in the near foreground, the glowing city across the valley sharp beyond him.

Shot 7: locked wide shot, the knight small and slumped at the base of the tree, the whole valley and the violet city around him, the scene finally still.

Throughout: pale mist drifts slowly across the valley, the light in the distant city flickers and breathes, wind moves the leaves and the torn cape, the knight's shoulders fall with each laboured breath. The camera stays locked in every shot, with at most an almost imperceptible push-in. No text, no lettering, no watermark.




Settings I use on that generation: 16:9, 19 seconds, NO music, only sounds effects. The music is a separate layer, added in post, so that the picture can be re-cut against the track without regenerating anything.







Post Production : DaVinci Resolve

AI is getting better but is not enough when it comes to an exact type of grain, chromatic aberration and color correction can cost a new generation, which we do not want. That's why I still use DaVinci Resolve to have the exact type of final render I want. 17 years of compositing in the cinema industry taught me how to handle AI errors, what I can do in post production and what AI can generate good.




I add my 35mm grain, chromatic aberration, 2 types of glow, vignette, a luma flickering to make it more 1990's movie, a little camera shake like old movies and of course a subtle color correction with a white clamp so nothing is overexposed.
