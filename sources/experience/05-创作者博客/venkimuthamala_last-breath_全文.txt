LAST BREATH: A Film About the Price of Air

By M.SHARATH CHAND— a short film made for the Higgsfield Film Contest

Imagine walking down a street where every person carries their air on their back. Steel cylinders, rubber tubes, small round gauges ticking toward zero. Nobody panics when a needle hits red, because everyone has seen what comes next. A man sinks to his knees, the crowd flows around him like water around a stone, and the city keeps walking.

That is the world of LAST BREATH, and it started with a single uncomfortable question: what if the thing we never think about, the air itself, became a product?

The Idea

Most dystopian stories are about scarcity of something we already know how to price: water, food, fuel. I wanted to go one step further. Breathing is the one act no one can opt out of, which makes it the perfect lever for a villain. If you control the air, you control everyone.

So the film imagines a planet that collapsed under its own pollution. Nothing grows. The sky is a permanent brown-grey haze. One industrialist has built the only functioning oxygen farm left, a guarded glass dome of engineered plants, and he sells the world its next breath one cylinder at a time. He doesn't see himself as a monster. He sees himself as a businessman.

Then a group of scientists discovers a plant that could undo all of it.

The Story (Spoiler-Light)

In a hidden underground lab, scientists find a small, glowing plant that can purify poisoned soil, spread on its own, and fill the air with oxygen within hours of being planted. It is free. It belongs to no one. And it would destroy the industrialist's empire overnight.

Before the lab is raided, one of the scientists entrusts the plant to a man he trusts. He asks only that the man protect it and plant it in the ground. The man promises, even if it costs him his life.

From there, the film becomes a chase. A lone man, a cloth-wrapped case held tight against his chest, hunted through neon markets and across rooftops by armed enforcers sent by the industrialist. He nearly loses the case mid-leap between two buildings. He is wounded. He never once becomes the aggressor.

What the enforcers don't know, until the very end, is what he has been carrying. And when he finally opens the case, one of the men hunting him is forced to decide who he really is.

I'm deliberately leaving the ending out of this post. Watch the film.

The Characters

The Hero. An ordinary man in a worn olive tactical jacket, not a super-soldier. His defining trait is what he does when he's hurt: in the moment he is shot, his hand goes to the case before it goes to his wound. That single gesture says everything about his priorities without a word of explanation.

The Gang Leader. A masked enforcer in matte-black armor with a glowing blue visor. On the surface, a cold instrument of the villain's will. Underneath, someone carrying a private grief that explains why he, of all the men on that rooftop, is the one who hesitates.

The Industrialist. Silver-haired, immaculately dressed, calm to the point of chill. He never raises his voice. He gives the order to destroy the plant with a single raised hand, as casually as approving a quarterly report. His menace comes from how ordinary he finds it.

The Scientists. Two men, one elder and one younger, who hold the film's emotional origin. They are the only characters who speak.

A Film That (Mostly) Doesn't Talk

I set myself a constraint at the start: no dialogue. Every story beat, who the hero is, who the villains are, why the chase is happening, had to be readable from imagery alone. If someone watched the film with the sound off in a language they didn't speak, they should still understand it.

To make that work, I built a simple visual grammar and stuck to it:

Warm light means hope. The hero, the case, and the plant are the only warm-lit things in a world of cold blues and greys.
Cold light means control. The enforcers, the villain, and the city itself live in steel blue, with harsh red for danger.
Faces tell you who to trust. The hero's face is always visible and human. The enforcers wear visors. Masks dehumanize; a bare face asks for sympathy.
The plant is always the brightest thing in frame. Wherever it appears, your eye goes to it before you know what it is.

I broke my own no-dialogue rule exactly once, in the lab scene where the scientists explain the plant and the hero makes his promise. It felt right to let the film speak at the single moment where a vow had to be heard rather than inferred. Everything after that returns to silence.

The Themes

Protection over violence. Even in a film with gunfights, the hero never kills. When he's forced to take a weapon, he disarms and disables. The film argues quietly that what separates him from his hunters isn't strength, it's restraint.

What people become when survival is a commodity. The crowds in the film aren't villains. They're exhausted, numb, and practiced at looking away, because looking would be unbearable. The villain didn't just sell air; he sold them a world where grief became routine.

Mercy as recognition. The rooftop standoff isn't won by force. It's resolved when someone sees something real and decides he can't unsee it.

Building the Film

LAST BREATH was assembled from short generated clips, roughly ten seconds each, planned shot by shot. I mapped the full runtime into a clip-by-clip structure first, then built a consistent set of reference images for the hero, gang leader, enforcers, villain, props like the case and plant, and each key location, so every clip drew from the same visual identity.

A few practical lessons from the process:

Reference consistency matters more than any single prompt. Reusing the same character and prop references across clips is what makes a generated film feel like one film.
Plan your geography. In the rooftop jump, I wrote the prompt so the takeoff point, the landing point, and the equal height of both buildings are always visible. Audiences forgive almost anything except confusion about where things are.
Vary the camera deliberately. Repeating the same angle across consecutive clips flattens the energy. Mixing low trackers, overhead inserts, slow motion, and handheld close-ups keeps the chase feeling alive.
Write around what filters will flag. When a prompt for a fall or an injury was rejected, the fix wasn't to weaken the emotion, it was to show consequence through stillness, dust, and breath rather than graphic detail. The restrained version was better anyway.
Sound is half the film. The score moves from cold minimalist electronics during the world-building, to kinetic hybrid percussion for the chase, to sparse solo piano for the flashback, and finally to warm choral and orchestral textures only once the plant is revealed. The choir appears nowhere in the film until that moment, so that when it arrives, it carries the whole weight of hope.
The World, Shot by Shot

The film opens from orbit. Earth, but wrong: grey-brown continents, a murky ocean, thick uneven bands of smog where white clouds should be. The camera descends through the haze into a vast city, past smokestacks pouring black smoke into the sky, down to street level where masked citizens queue at a fortified window to buy air.

Then we meet the man who owns it, walking calmly through rows of lush green plants inside his sealed dome, a stark contrast to the dead world visible through the glass behind him. When an alert reaches him, he doesn't flinch. He lifts one hand and gives the order.

And somewhere in an alley, a man with a glowing case is already running.

Why This Story

I wanted to make something that felt big in scale but small in its emotional core. The film's entire argument fits in one image: a single tiny light, held in cupped hands, in a world that has forgotten what green looks like.

If there's one idea I hope people leave with, it's this: the most radical thing a person can do in a system built on scarcity is give something away for free.

Watch, Share, Breathe

LAST BREATH is my entry for the Higgsfield Film Contest. If it moved you, or even just made you notice your own breathing for a second, that's the film doing its job.
