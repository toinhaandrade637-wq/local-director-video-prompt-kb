Twenty-five seconds. Eight shots. 4:3. Every frame generated in Seedance 2.5 from text only: no reference images, no keyframes, no image-to-video, no character sheets. The result looks like someone filmed an oil painting on a camcorder in the middle of a block party. This post walks through how it was built, what I locked and what I let go of, and gives you a simplified version of every prompt so you can run the same idea yourself.

The idea

The verse is about a crew, dreams, and what money does to your head. I did not want to illustrate the lyrics line by line. Instead I built a set of ordinary neighborhood scenes and gave each one a single surreal flex: gold chains instead of Halloween candy, a horse in high-top sneakers, a school bus made of solid gold, a stingray drifting through a subway car, a chain ceremony on the sidewalk, a water-gun standoff with a cop car. One image per shot, two to four seconds each, hard cuts, no transitions.

Before writing a single prompt I set a few rules and never broke them:

The world is one specific neighborhood in a US city, and everyone in frame belongs to it, down to the extras and the reflections in the windows.
Street style and swagger first, surrealism second. Nothing glossy, nothing that looks like a car commercial.
The camera is a friend with a camcorder. Handheld, eye level, small shake, quick optical zooms.
No VHS filters, no glitch, no datamosh. The texture comes from paint, not from fake tape damage.
The look: three textures boiled into one paragraph

I wanted painting, not CGI. Three textures were in my head:

Oil on canvas: thick brushstrokes, impasto, faces chopped out of three or four color strokes, a poisonous palette sitting on deep black.
Night neon: stretched long-limbed figures, a city painted in light rather than in geometry.
Comic-book print: halftone dots in the shadows, ink outlines, slight CMYK misregistration, motion that feels like it is animated on twos.

The important decision was to not name any film, studio or artist in the prompt. I described the techniques in plain words. That transfers more reliably than hoping the model knows a title, and it gives you control over which part of a look you actually want. This became the style block that opens every single prompt in the project:

Hand-painted animation. Thick oil brushstrokes and impasto texture, faces cut out of a few bold color strokes, halftone dots and ink outlines in the shadows, slight CMYK misregistration on the edges, motion that feels animated on twos. Poison-bright palette of magenta, acid green, teal and polished gold sitting on deep black. Night neon or one harsh light source. Handheld home-video camera held by a friend: small shake, quick optical zooms, eye level. Black neighborhood in a US city, all-Black cast including extras. No glitch, no VHS, no CGI gloss, no readable text. 4:3.

Everything below is written as "STYLE BLOCK + scene". In the actual generation the paragraph was pasted in full every time, word for word. Repetition is the consistency mechanism when you have no reference images.

Why text-to-video only

This was the biggest bet. The usual way to keep a look consistent across shots is to build assets first: character sheets, location plates, a style frame, then animate from those. I skipped all of it.

Three reasons:

Image-to-video tends to leave a "photo pasted into a painting" feeling. A locked first frame fights the brushstroke motion instead of feeding it.
The video is a neighborhood, not a protagonist. Different faces in every shot are a feature. What has to stay consistent is the paint, the palette, the camera and the light, and all of that lives in words.
Speed. Eight shots, one prompt each, no asset stage.

What holds it together: the same style paragraph verbatim, the same five color words, the same camera sentence, the same aspect ratio. Seedance 2.5 at 1080p in 4:3. I generated short clips and cut them down to the best two to four seconds in the edit.

The edit

The track is a dusty boom-bap loop, so the cut is boom-bap too: hard cuts landing on the kick, two to four seconds per shot, no transitions, no speed ramps. Each clip was generated longer than needed and I kept the best stretch. The order is not the order I generated them in; I arranged them as an escalation of flexes and then dropped the tempo for the car-wash closer. The title card lives on top of the last shot and then holds on black for a beat after the picture goes.

What I learned
Seedance will invent lettering. The table sign in shot 6 came out as gibberish, which I kept because it reads as texture in a painting. If you cannot live with that, "no readable text" in the style block cuts it down a lot.
"Handheld home-video camera held by a friend" was the single most valuable sentence in the project. The shake, the eye level and the optical zooms all came from it, and it is what makes the paint feel filmed rather than rendered.
Guard against gloss every time. Without "no CGI gloss" in the block, gold and puffer jackets slide toward a plastic render look.
One gag per shot, one action per shot. Seedance handles "the door opens, he drops the gold" far better than a chain of five beats. If the scene needs more, split it into two clips and cut.
Deep black backgrounds are your friend. They hide the model's weak spots, make the neon and gold pop, and match the impasto look where the canvas simply stops.
Describe technique, not titles. "Faces cut out of a few bold color strokes" gets you the painted look more reliably than naming a painter or a film.
You do not need a protagonist to have consistency. Palette, light, camera and texture carry the world; the people can change.
Try it yourself

Take the style block above, keep it word for word, and write one plain sentence of place, one of people, and one of action underneath it. Set 4:3, 1080p, Seedance 2.5, text-to-video. Generate a few takes, keep the best three seconds of each, cut on the kick. That is the whole method.
