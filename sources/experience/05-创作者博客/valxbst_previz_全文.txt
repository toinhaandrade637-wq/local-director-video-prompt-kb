Why block it in Blender first

A camera move costs nothing in Blender and 195 credits in Seedance. So the move gets built and approved for free, and the generation only ever pays for the look.

The previz is grey boxes and a camera path — 30 seconds, 24fps, 9:16, rendered straight out of the viewport. It carries no lighting, no materials and no detail. It exists to answer one question: does the camera do the right thing at the right time.

Blender talks to Higgsfield through the Bridge plugin, and Claude drives it — the blocking gets briefed in plain English rather than modelled by hand. If you have not set that up yet, adilinthewild's walkthrough is the one to follow:

https://higgsfield.ai/@adilinthewild/blogs/this-blender-higgsfield-ai-workflow-changes-how-you-make-ai-video 







The colour legend

This is the part that makes it work, and almost nobody publishes it. The blockout is colour-coded and the legend is declared inside the video prompt, so the model can read the previz:

RED tall block        = the hero. Wherever the red block is, she is.
WHITE panel on front  = the direction she is FACING.
TAN block on top      = her head.
SMALL DARK BOX        = the handheld camera in her hands.
RED PANEL ON A STEM   = the same hero, marking where she stands in the vertical rises.
GREY blocks           = anonymous strangers. Never characters, never featured.

Then one line that stops the previz leaking into the look:

Nothing in the finished film is red, white or grey because the previz was. The legend is read and then discarded.

And the clause that settles every conflict:

Where @Video 1 and any other reference disagree, @Video 1 always wins — on angle, on position, on timing. Any deviation from the path in @Video 1 is a failure.
Why the wardrobe is ivory

This is a measurement rather than a taste call. The film is roughly 60% blue dusk, so the hero's wardrobe is a luminance decision before it is a colour one — a saturated red or navy is colourful and still dark, and sinks into every background. Ivory rather than pure white, because white clips under the lanterns and in screen light and loses the pattern exactly when she is biggest in frame. Measured on the final plate: garment reads 223 against a background of 145–173. She is the lightest thing in any frame she is in.

The indigo obi turned out to matter more than expected — downsampled to the size she occupies in a wide shot, the pattern goes soft but the obi holds as a hard dark band at her waist. A strong horizontal value break is what keeps a costume readable at distance.

Three things worth knowing

Never name a single garment inside a wardrobe reference's own line. Name the reference, say what the person is doing, and say nothing about the cloth. One concrete item in that sentence and the model stops using the element and starts building an outfit around the item instead.

Keep every beat the same length. Word count is screen-time weighting whether you intend it or not — the longest beat takes the most seconds. Beats here run 54–93 words with a 1.7× spread between the shortest and longest, and lighting lives in one block above the timing rather than inside the beats, so the schedule stays visible.

Write what is there, never what is absent. This model cannot play a negation — it plays the action and drops the "not". "She does not board the train" gets you her boarding it. Write the positive instead: she stays on the platform all five seconds, feet never moving. And when a negation would have to fight a strong prior, remove the noun altogether rather than negating it — "cherry trees, not in blossom" under strung lanterns in Japan will give you blossom every time, so the species simply isn't named.
