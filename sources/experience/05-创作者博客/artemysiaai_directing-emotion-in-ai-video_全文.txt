DIRECTING EMOTION IN AI VIDEO
A
Sophie Nardi
@artemysiaai
33
1
1

AI can now generate almost any world we imagine. That's exactly why the world is no longer enough. What makes a video stay with someone is the emotion on a face, and that's something we still have to direct, shot by shot.

The gaze: where a character looks is the story

The eyes are the first thing I direct, before the movements. Where a character looks tells the audience what matters to them in that moment.

Looking at someone creates connection, tension or a challenge, depending on how long the look lasts.
Looking away says shame, fear, a secret, or a love that can't be spoken.
Looking down and then up again is a decision. It's often the most powerful beat in a scene.
An empty stare into the distance is memory, loss, a mind somewhere else.

In a two-character scene, the gaze is also how you build the relationship without dialogue. 










One practical rule: the eyes need life. A fixed, glassy stare is the fastest way to make an AI character feel artificial. Small eye movements and a natural blink make the difference between a doll and a person.




Micro-expressions: small movements, big emotions

Real emotion rarely looks like a big smile or a dramatic scream. It lives in tiny movements: a jaw that tightens, lips that part and close again, a breath held a second too long, eyes that start to shine before the tears come.

These are the details I look for in every generation:

Contradiction. A character who smiles while their eyes are sad is far more interesting than one who is simply happy.
Transition. The best shots show an emotion changing, like anger melting into pain. A face that moves from one state to another feels alive.
Restraint. Holding back is often stronger than showing everything. A tear that doesn't fall can hit harder than open crying.
The body follows the face. Shoulders that drop, a hand that closes into a fist, a step back. Emotion doesn't stop at the neck.

When an expression is too big, the audience sees acting. When it's small and true, they feel it.







The edit: giving emotion time to land

A great expression can be ruined by a bad cut. As a video editor, this is where I protect the emotion I fought for in the generations.

Close-ups for the turning points. Wide shots show the world. Close-ups show the heart. I save the tightest framing for the moments that change the story.
Hold the shot. The instinct is to cut fast. But an emotion needs a second or two to reach the audience. Sometimes the strongest moment is the silence after a look.
Cut on the eyes. I often cut right after a glance, so the next shot shows what the character is looking at, or how the other person reacts.
Let the music breathe with the face. I create my own music, and I time it to the emotion, not only to the action. A beat drop on a tear, a pause in the music on a held look.

The edit decides when the audience feels something. Rhythm is emotion too.







The technology is the brush. The emotion is the painting.

Every month the tools get better. Faces are more realistic, movements more natural, details sharper. That's exciting, but it also means that soon everyone will have access to beautiful images.

What will make the difference is the same thing that always has: the ability to make someone feel something. A look that says everything. A silence that weighs more than a line of dialogue.

So next time you create a scene, before you think about the world around your character, ask yourself one question: what are their eyes saying?
