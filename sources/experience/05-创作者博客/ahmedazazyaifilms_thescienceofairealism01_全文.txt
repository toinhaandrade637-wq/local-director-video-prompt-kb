In a 2025 study, people looked at real faces next to faces made with GPT-4o and picked the real one only 43% of the time. That is worse than flipping a coin. A larger Microsoft experiment, with more than 12,500 people and about 287,000 ratings, landed at 62%. And in 2026, people watching Veo 3.1 videos next to real footage were right 66% of the time.






I make AI ads for a living, and those numbers changed how I review my own work. If people can't tell, "I feel it's AI" is no longer a method. You need a checklist.

The eye doesn't measure quality



Rob Horning wrote in Aperture in early 2025 that the idea of a "real photo" doesn't really hold up. Every image is a representation. Every video gets cut, graded and composited, and we still call it real. What the eye trusts is not accuracy. It trusts the conventions it is used to. When something breaks those conventions, the eye files it as fake.



That is also why the uncanny valley still matters. The closer something gets to human without being human, the stronger the rejection, and the valley is deeper for video than for stills, because motion gives more away. A 2025 review of 29 studies found a clear valley in only 9 of them, so treat it as a useful lens, not a law.



The four layers I check

I break every frame into four layers and check each one on its own.

1. The character
- Skin: random pores, uneven color, personal marks, the same age on face, neck and hands, and light that changes the skin.


- Face: break the average. Natural teeth, no perfect symmetry, stray hairs, eye reflections that match the light.


- Expression: tone it down. In a 2026 study, GPT-4o expressions were rated more intense than real ones, 6.40 vs 5.77. Real expressions build slowly, peak briefly and settle slowly.


- Eyes: viewers look there first. In a 2025 eye-tracking study, fixations averaged 10.6 on the eyes, 5.8 on the mouth and 1.6 on the body. Blinking also drops when the brain works harder, so a character that never blinks, or blinks like a clock, gives itself away. 


- Body and hands: weight, anticipation before every move, nothing stopping at once, turns that start with the eyes, and one action per hand. 


2. The camera
No real camera moves, stops and moves again. Keep the lens, depth of field and grain natural, and give every light a source. 


3. The world
A perfect location looks plastic. Wrinkled clothes, fingerprints on glass, a cup ring on the table. Wind and weather should leave marks, and the place should stay the same from shot to shot. 


4. The words
The words we add to look realistic often push the model toward the plastic look: hyperrealistic, 8K render, Unreal Engine, perfect symmetry. Write natural imperfection and describe the lens instead. Avoid brand names, since some models reject them. And direct with verbs, not adjectives: "her eyes crinkle half a second before her mouth" works better than "she looks embarrassed". 


One craft rule from my own work: models tend to drift after about 4 to 5 seconds, so I cut before that and come back.

The point: AI gives you the average. You make the exception. Realism isn't resolution. Realism is consistency: everything in the frame has to agree with everything else, because the eye is always looking for the first contradiction.
