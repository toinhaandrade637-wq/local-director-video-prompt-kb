Give Astra the Complete Assignment

The brief needs to define the question, format, duration, and review point. Leave room for Astra to propose the framing and sequence of visual reveals.




What it does: Asks Astra to research the explanation, develop the narration and scene plan, and propose a visual direction for a thirty-second explainer. Review that proposal before production begins.




Copy into Codex:

Create a thirty-second, sixteen-by-nine Vox-style explainer about why traffic jams happen without an accident. Use my motion graphics Skill and Higgsfield, with three ten-second blocks and English narration. Research the explanation, develop the script and visual direction, and produce one finished video. Show me the proposed script, scene plan, and style direction before production.

The explanation should make one mechanism clear: under the right traffic conditions, a small change in speed can grow into a larger disturbance as drivers react behind it. Experiments have demonstrated that jams can form without a bottleneck. See the discussion and evidence in Traffic Experiment Reveals the Nature of Car-Following.




For the animation, the key distinction is direction: the cars travel forward, while the slowdown can spread backward through traffic. Check that Astra's proposed narration and visuals communicate both movements.

Stage 2 — Build the Visual Foundation
Create One Shared Visual Reference

Before producing all three scenes, review Astra's treatment of the cars, road, background, and typography.




The cars need to remain recognizable as the camera pulls back. Their contrast against the road should make changes in spacing easy to see. Background texture should support the composition without obscuring the movement that carries the explanation.




Keep the palette, textures, and car design in a shared style block, with the action described separately for each shot. This gives Astra a consistent starting point when a scene needs a wider view or a different composition.

Establish the Typography

Define the type hierarchy in the visual reference: larger text for the main idea, smaller labels for details such as the first car slowing down.




Keep readable text out of the generated footage and add the actual captions and labels during assembly. That makes their wording, placement, and timing easier to adjust after the animation is in place.




Use those roles consistently across the three scenes. A label that identifies the initiating slowdown should be easy to distinguish from a headline introducing the overall explanation.

Stage 3 — Produce and Refine the Scenes
Scene 1 — Set Up the Mystery

The opening ten seconds need to establish the question: traffic slows or stops, yet there is no visible accident explaining it.




Follow the approved scene plan and give the viewer a clear starting point. The opening should introduce the road and traffic in the shared visual style, then lead naturally into the mechanism shown in the central scene.




Although this scene appears first in the edit, produce the central slowdown scene first. It is the most useful early test of whether the visual approach can explain the subject.

Scene 2 — Make the Slowdown Visible

The central ten seconds carry the hardest visual task: connecting one driver's change in speed to a wider traffic pattern.




A useful approach is to begin close enough to notice the first car slowing down, then widen the view as drivers behind it react. Review Astra's proposed framing and order of movement before deciding whether that approach works for the scene.

Style header: Reuse the approved palette, textures, and car design at the top of each scene's production prompt. Keep music for the final assembly so the sequence can share one continuous soundtrack.




Watch the preview twice. First, follow the spacing between the cars. Then follow where braking begins and how the reaction travels through the line. The scene should let you identify the initiating action and understand the larger pattern as the view opens up.




Save this version before requesting a revision.

Scene 3 — Complete the Explanation

Use the final ten seconds to make the distinction between vehicle movement and the traffic wave easy to understand. Carry forward the same visual treatment and connect the ending to the approved narration.




Once the central scene works, produce the opening and ending, then assemble all three against the voiceover.




Plan transitions around where the viewer is looking. If one shot directs attention to a particular car, keeping that car in roughly the same part of the next frame can help the viewer follow the change in view.




Play each transition with the narration. Check that the explanation remains easy to follow through the cut, then save the complete first version.

Give Astra a Specific Client Revision

A useful revision tests whether Astra can change the emphasis while preserving the structure of the video.




Here, the client wants viewers to spend a little longer noticing the first driver slowing down. The scene still has only ten seconds, so the extra time has to come from somewhere else.




What it does: Asks Astra to give the initiating slowdown about one extra second, propose where to recover that time, and preserve the narration, duration, style, and neighboring scenes.




Copy into Codex:

Revise the slowdown scene to emphasize the initiating action. Keep the first car's slowdown clearly visible for about one extra second before expanding attention to the wider traffic pattern. Propose where to recover that time within the same scene. Preserve the narration, total scene duration, visual style, and neighboring scenes. Keep the original version for comparison.




Review the timing proposal before producing the alternative. A hold at the end may offer room to recover the second. Shortening that hold could preserve the pace of the chain reaction while giving the first action more breathing room.

The right choice depends on the actual preview. If there is no expendable hold, examine another part of the scene before approving the change.

Compare the Two Versions

Play the original and revision from the same starting point with the same narration. Check three things:

Is the first driver's slowdown easier to notice?
Is the reaction through the other cars still clear?
Does each version finish at ten seconds?

Choose the revision only if it meets the client's note and preserves the explanation. Then watch it between the opening and ending scenes to check the rhythm of the full sequence.

Finish the Typography, Sound, and Export

Add labels where they clarify the action. A short label can identify the first slowdown; a directional marker can distinguish the cars' movement from the traffic wave. Keep the wording consistent with the narration.




Give each label enough time to be read. Check its entrance against the camera movement and the moment the viewer's attention reaches that part of the frame.

For sound, listen to the narration alone before adding music and effects. Lower the music under speech, and keep any braking effect subtle enough that the words explaining the moment remain clear.




Finally, export the video and play the exported file all the way through. Confirm the thirty-second runtime, the revised scene's continuity, readable text, and clear narration. The export is the deliverable to review before handing the video to a client.
