Project statement

With the Higgsfield Global Film Festival deadline now extended to September 14, I’ve made the decision to remake Count of Three from the ground up.

The original version was the first short film I had ever made, and the process taught me far more than I expected — not only about AI filmmaking, but about visual storytelling, pacing, character, performance, cinematography and what separates an impressive sequence from a film that actually stays with you.

I reached a point where I realised I could continue polishing the existing film, or use everything I had learned to make something significantly better.

I’ve chosen the latter.

The scale, action and technical ambition are still there, but the new version is much more focused on character, visual authorship and a single clear idea at its centre. I’m rebuilding the childhood, environments, character design, narrative structure and visual language rather than being constrained by decisions I made when I was still learning how to make the film.

The first version proved to me what I could make.

The aim now is to make the film I would have made if I knew at the beginning what I know now.

Project brief

Count of Three is an intimate science-fiction drama about two childhood friends separated by an unstoppable advancing wall.

The wall does not merely divide the world. Everything it passes through is physically destroyed.

Humanity has found one way to survive: enter the wall.

Those absorbed by it are physically sustained while living within an artificial reality capable of providing safety, comfort and seemingly perfect lives.

Most people eventually choose it.

Moss does not.

After losing his parents to the wall as a child, and later losing his closest friend Eli to it as well, Moss spends decades travelling ahead of its advance — rebuilding, leaving, travelling and rebuilding again.

Eventually the wall reaches the end of the physical world.

There is nowhere left to run.

And when it arrives one final time, Eli comes out of it.

✨ Context

This project is being created for the Higgsfield Global Film Festival.

The film uses AI filmmaking to create a world and scale that would ordinarily demand an enormous physical production, but the intention is increasingly the opposite of spectacle for spectacle’s sake.

The world may be enormous.

The story is not.

At its centre are two people, one friendship, and a question neither of them can answer for the other:

What makes a life real?

AI is being used not simply to generate large environments and action sequences, but to build a coherent cinematic language around memory, physical reality, impermanence and the strange ways people survive losing things.

🎭 Narrative

Moss grows up outside the wall.

His parents are already inside it.

As a child he meets Eli, another lonely boy who briefly becomes the closest thing he has to family. They play in fields, build crude shelters from whatever they find, fight with wooden sticks and invent a game that always ends with the same count:

One. Two. Three.

Then Eli is taken by the wall too.

Moss remains outside.

Years pass.

The wall continues advancing.

Moss moves from settlement to settlement, growing older as landscapes, homes and communities disappear behind him.

Along the way he meets some of the increasingly rare people who have also chosen to remain in physical reality.

They do not all agree with Moss.

Some enjoy travelling.

Some eventually become tired of rebuilding and voluntarily enter the wall.

Some stay outside simply because they have already left too many places.

Through them, the film does not present Moss as the person who has discovered the correct answer. He is simply one person responding to an impossible situation in the only way he knows how.

Meanwhile, Eli has grown up inside the wall.

Life there is not presented as torture.

It is beautiful.

Safe.

Stable.

The people inside genuinely experience their lives as lives.

Eli has therefore grown up with an entirely different relationship to the wall. To him, it is not simply the thing that destroys the physical world. It is also the thing that saved him.

Eventually Moss reaches the final settlement at the edge of the remaining physical world.

Beyond it there is nowhere else to travel.

For the first time in his life, Moss builds knowing that he cannot simply leave again.

Then the wall arrives.

Most of the remaining people accept evacuation into it.

Moss refuses.

And among the people who emerge to bring the final inhabitants inside is adult Eli.

Their reunion begins as an attempt to save one another.

Each simply disagrees about what saving someone means.

Eli believes Moss will die if he remains outside.

Moss believes entering the wall would mean surrendering the very thing he has spent his life protecting.

Their confrontation becomes physical.

And gradually, the adult fight begins repeating the game they played together as children.

Movements align across decades.

A wrist is caught the same way.

A fall becomes a childhood tumble through grass.

A strike becomes a wooden stick.

The past begins appearing inside the present.

What initially looks like two adult men fighting at the end of the world slowly reveals itself as something far smaller:

two children who never finished saying goodbye.

🧠 Themes

The new version of Count of Three is fundamentally about reality, loss, memory and the ways human beings respond to the knowledge that everything ends.

The wall offers an apparently extraordinary solution to suffering.

You do not need to lose your home.

You do not need to grow hungry.

You do not need to remain cold.

You do not necessarily even need to lose the people you love.

But the experience exists inside something constructed.

Moss rejects that bargain.

Eli lives inside it.

Neither is treated as obviously correct.

If a person inside the wall loves, remembers, grieves, laughs and experiences a lifetime, on what basis can someone outside declare that life meaningless?

At the same time, if every painful element of reality can simply be replaced by something safer, editable and permanent, what happens to our relationship with things precisely because they cannot be preserved forever?

The film therefore asks rather than answers:

If an artificial life feels completely real, what exactly makes it less real?

If everything physical disappears eventually, does that make preserving it pointless?

Is avoiding pain worth avoiding the possibility of genuine loss?

And beneath all of those questions is something much simpler:

What do we carry when everything else is taken away?

For Moss, one of those things is Eli.

For Eli, despite everything the wall gave him, it was Moss too.

👦 Childhood

Childhood is no longer intended primarily as a beautiful montage.

It is being rebuilt as a genuine dramatic section of the film.

The boys should feel as though they actually spent time together.

They play.

They annoy each other.

They build things badly.

They fight.

They laugh.

They sit around doing almost nothing.

Small behaviours should establish their relationship before the film ever asks the audience to mourn it.

The childhood photography is tactile and imperfect:

strong natural backlight, overexposed edges, grass obscuring faces, scratched knees, dirty clothing, skin texture, imperfect focus, awkward framing, long observational takes and moments that do not obviously advance the plot.

The aim is for the audience not merely to understand that Eli and Moss were friends, but to feel:

I know these two.

Their childhood game later becomes the physical language of the adult confrontation.

🧱 The wall

The wall is inevitable.

It continues moving.

It cannot be defeated.

Anything physical behind its advance is eventually gone.

The majority of humanity has therefore stopped trying to outrun it.

People enter.

The minority who remain outside continue moving ahead of it for as long as possible.

The wall should remain visually mysterious and monumental, but its narrative function is simple enough to understand very early.

It is both:

destruction

and

salvation.

For Moss, it took his family.

For Eli, it gave him another life.

The film should never completely reconcile those two truths.

👤 The Emissary

The Emissary remains in the film, but its role is smaller and more precise.

It is no longer primarily a philosophical teacher.

It functions as something closer to an interface between a person and the world inside the wall.

When young Eli enters, the Emissary can offer him something impossible:

the field he remembers,

the world he lost,

perhaps even an approximation of Moss.

The scene establishes a central property of the wall:

it can reproduce experience extraordinarily well.

But Eli can still sense the difference between something being recreated and something having actually continued.

The Emissary does not need to explain what this means.

The audience can decide.

🚶 Moss's journey

A significant part of the film follows Moss across time.

The same wall appears behind him at different ages.

Child Moss moves.

Teenage Moss moves.

Young adult Moss moves.

Adult Moss moves.

Settlements change.

Clothing changes.

People change.

Certain objects remain.

The repetition itself becomes storytelling.

During these travels Moss encounters some of the tiny minority who have chosen to remain outside the wall.

Their perspectives should complicate rather than validate his own.

Someone might tell him:

“You're always looking backwards.”

Someone voluntarily entering the wall might simply say:

“I'm tired of starting again.”

An older person choosing to stop travelling might say:

“I've left enough places.”

These are not philosophers delivering the film's message.

They are ordinary people who have independently reached different answers to the same impossible circumstance.

Moss listens.

Then keeps moving.

🌍 The end of the world

Eventually the remaining physical land runs out.

The final settlement exists at a genuine geographic terminus — beyond it there is no meaningful place to continue travelling.

People who have spent generations avoiding the wall gradually accumulate there.

As a result, the settlement contains fragments of dozens of lost places.

Architecture is assembled from salvaged material.

Different building traditions overlap.

Fabric is repaired.

Timber has been reused several times.

Objects carry evidence of previous lives.

It is not a civilisation designed around an abstract philosophy.

It is simply what happens when generations of displaced people keep carrying pieces of disappearing places forward.

For the first time in Moss's life, he builds somewhere knowing:

there will not be another settlement after this one.

Then the wall appears.

⚔️ The final confrontation

The fight remains a major part of the film.

But its meaning has changed.

Eli is not trying to conquer Moss.

He is trying to prevent him from dying.

Moss is not trying to defeat Eli's civilisation.

He is refusing the only form of survival Eli can offer him.

Both believe the other is making a catastrophic mistake.

That gives the violence its tragedy.

As the fight continues, the enormous context begins disappearing.

The wall.

The evacuation.

The settlement.

The crowds.

Eventually even the weapons become secondary.

Childhood begins bleeding into the confrontation through match cuts.

Adult Moss swings.

Child Moss swings.

Adult Eli catches him.

Child Eli catches him.

Snow becomes grass.

A violent fall becomes two children laughing on the ground.

The further the fight progresses, the harder it becomes to separate the men they became from the children they were.

The choreography therefore carries narrative rather than existing separately from it.

🎨 Visuals

The visual approach is also being rebuilt.

The new goal is not simply cinematic.

It is authored.

Frames should feel discovered rather than perfectly generated.

Characters may be partially obscured by grass, fabric, beams, snow, windows or other people.

Focus may occasionally fall slightly imperfectly.

Highlights may blow out.

Faces do not always need to be clearly presented.

Clothing should feel worn rather than costumed.

Objects should have history.

The physical world should feel touched.

Outside the wall

Tactile.

Unrepeatable.

Weathered.

Dirty.

Wet.

Windblown.

Repaired.

Imperfect.

Materials physically age.

Things acquire history.

Inside the wall

Beautiful.

Calm.

Controlled.

Possibly even more visually desirable.

But strangely free from irreversible deterioration.

Surfaces can remain immaculate.

Environments can be changed.

Damage does not necessarily need to persist.

The distinction is therefore not:

ugly reality / beautiful fake world.

It is:

irreversible reality / editable reality.

That is a much more interesting visual contrast.

🎥 Cinematography

The camera should increasingly resist conventional AI-film imagery.

The photography favours:

child-height perspectives
long lenses
physical foreground obstruction
silhouettes
backlight
real atmospheric depth
negative space
imperfect asymmetry
characters entering and leaving compositions rather than always being followed
natural handheld micro-movement
tactile close-ups of hands, fabric, wood, skin, snow and water
shots that linger after the apparent action has finished

Large unobstructed spectacle shots should be relatively rare.

That makes them powerful when they occur.

The film should be capable of feeling enormous and extremely intimate within seconds of each other.

The target is:

grand and niche simultaneously.

🎬 Production

The film is being produced primarily through AI-generated imagery and video, but the new production approach is considerably more deliberate than the first version.

Rather than generating attractive sequences and discovering their purpose afterwards, the new workflow begins with:

script → theme → character → production design → visual grammar → storyboard → generation.

Character identities, clothing, environments, objects and ageing will be designed before full scene generation begins.

Each location will have a clear visual philosophy.

Each costume will communicate character history.

Each recurring object will have continuity.

Complex action will continue to be broken into short controlled generations, but the priority is no longer simply technical continuity.

The goal is thematic continuity as well.

🚀 Status

Following the extension of the Higgsfield Global Film Festival deadline to September 14, production has entered a deliberate rebuild.

A substantial first version of Count of Three had already been developed, including childhood material, extensive world design, the wall, Eli's society, Moss's settlement, evacuation sequences, action choreography and a large portion of the final confrontation.

That work has not been wasted.

It established the technical capabilities of the project and revealed what worked — and, more importantly, what could be significantly improved.

Rather than spend the additional time polishing decisions made during my first attempt at creating a short film, I am using what I learned to rebuild the project around a stronger script, clearer central concept and more distinctive visual language.

The immediate next stage is to lock:

the complete screenplay,
character designs,
age progression,
costume language,
the wall,
the physical world,
the world inside the wall,
the final settlement,
and the cinematographic rules governing each part of the story.

Generation of the new film will then proceed from that foundation.

📝 Notes

The guiding principle of the rebuilt Count of Three is simplicity beneath complexity.

The world can be enormous.

The imagery can be ambitious.

The action can be spectacular.

But the audience should understand the emotional engine almost immediately:

A boy loses the people he loves to an advancing wall. He spends his entire life staying ahead of it. When there is finally nowhere left to run, the last person the wall brings back to him is the friend he lost as a child.

Everything else grows from that.

The wall creates the deadline.

The journey creates Moss.

The world inside creates Eli.

The people Moss meets complicate the question.

The fight forces the question into the relationship.

And childhood gives all of it meaning.

By the end, the largest object in the film should once again become less important than the smallest ones:

a battered wooden stick,
three knocks on a door,
a movement remembered after twenty years,
and two children who once found each other when both of them were alone.
