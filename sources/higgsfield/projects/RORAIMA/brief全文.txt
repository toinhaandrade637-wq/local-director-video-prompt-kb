ABOUT THE PROJECT
The film

We’ve always been drawn to the mythic worlds of ancient civilizations and the way they understood themselves through nature. Man against the mountain, the desert, the sea – the crossing itself as the thing worth telling. Stories of human beings set against nature are rarely only about survival; the obstacle outside becomes a mirror of the struggle within.

Hemingway, Campbell and Miyazaki each explored this idea in radically different ways, and all three became touchstones for the project. From Hemingway: a man alone before a force indifferent to him, dignified by his refusal to quit. From Campbell: the hero’s journey as the story every culture tells in its own words. From Miyazaki: landscapes rendered so enormous and so beautiful that awe and terror cease to be separate feelings. We wanted to create a fictional folktale that could transcend culture and time – something beautiful, terrifying and deeply emotional, built around the human spirit’s capacity to confront what appears impossible.

And so we created RORAIMA.

In our fictional world, set within the ancient Guiana Shield, a man wins the woman he loves by climbing. He ascends one of the hills surrounding their village, plucks a flower from its summit, places it inside a clay urn she has painted for him, and carries it safely back to her. Every young man in the village has done it. Each month, the hills are alive with them – running, scrambling, tumbling into the grass, holding their urns aloft so they do not break. But beyond them all stands Roraima: a colossal, flat-topped wall vanishing into cloud, its waterfalls pouring over the rim and dissolving into mist before they ever reach the ground. Nobody climbs Roraima. Perhaps the Chief did, once.

At its simplest, the idea is almost funny: a man climbs a mountain to pick a flower for the woman he loves. But there is something primal and completely honest in that gesture. The flower is tiny; the effort is enormous. That imbalance is the romance. The flower is only the token; the distance is the gift. And so, while the other women receive flowers from the surrounding hills, the Chief’s daughter wants hers from Roraima. She believes she deserves no ordinary gesture – because, well, have you seen her?

The film opens on our hero, a young highland man, running through thick jungle at dusk with a clay urn held tight against his chest. He bursts into the open savanna and stops. Before him, Mount Roraima towers above the world: treacherous, immense and impossibly beautiful. We then return to the village and see how the challenge began. He sits beside the Chief’s daughter as she paints her urn. She has chosen no one yet. Together, they laugh at the commotion around them – the young men racing over the surrounding hills in pursuit of their flowers. Then she takes his hand and points beyond them, toward the greatest mountain of all: Roraima. Inside the Chief’s house sits an ancient urn containing a flower unlike any other: dried and fragile, yet still glowing with an ethereal light. In that moment, our hero understands what he must do to win her heart.

Then the climb begins. The ascent carries him from day into night, and from night into storm. Slick stone. A narrow fissure. A waterfall pounding against his back. A handhold breaking beneath his grip. A vine, a torn fingernail, blood running between his fingers. Every time he slips, he protects the urn before he protects himself. On the upper cliff, the sack tears. The urn falls. What is inside it re-reads every frame that came before it...

At its heart, RORAIMA is about the truth that the greatest mountains we climb are often the ones within us. Our hero begins by believing love is proved through distance and endurance: how far he is willing to climb, how fiercely he can hold on. Roraima teaches him something harder. The ascent requires him to hold on; reaching the summit requires him to let go. The physical climb and inner journey become two faces of the same impossible task. It is a mythic story, but we hope also a universal and deeply human one - about love beyond reward, the limits of conquest, and the discovery that some mountains are climbed not to defeat them, but to understand what must be left at the summit.

The visuals

We wanted the film to look painted. We chose a painterly form of animation – gouache brushwork laid over dimensional CG geometry, animated on twos within a 24-fps timeline – because it allowed the world to feel tactile, physical and dangerous without binding it to photographic realism. Visible brushwork, simplified shapes and matte surfaces make every frame feel touched by hand. Long before anyone could film an image, people used paint to preserve the things that mattered to them. A story about an ancient civilization felt like the perfect expression of that impulse!

Nature is never merely a backdrop. The rainforest, grasslands, rain, cloud and sheer stone all exert their own presence, while the human figures remain small within them. Roraima is not simply a location; it is the film’s dominant force – inviting and terrifying in the same image. The palette follows the journey from the warmth of village life into the deep blues and violets of night and storm, before finally emerging into the first light of dawn.

Color is treated as narrative, not decoration. Among the young women of the village, red belongs exclusively to the Chief’s daughter; everyone else wears undyed beige. Before the audience understands her place in the story, the palette tells us how our hero sees her: singular, vivid and impossible to overlook.

By contrast, we deliberately designed our hero to look like any other young man in the village. Nothing in his clothing, stature or position announces that he is exceptional. He is not dressed as a hero or marked by destiny; his distinction emerges entirely through what he chooses to do and what he is willing to endure. The extraordinary act belongs to an ordinary man.

That same restraint governs everything within the frame. With AI-generated imagery, visual abundance can easily be mistaken for design; we wanted every decision to feel authored. No texture, color, fabric, ornament or object remains simply because the model happened to produce something interesting. Every element is deliberately placed, repeated and controlled.

The materials obey the logic of the world: garments are woven or wrapped, pigments are drawn from the earth, and clay, rope and wood bear the evidence of human hands. By limiting the visual world to what could plausibly be gathered, shaped, woven or fired within this environment, we wanted its civilization to feel as though it had grown from the Guiana Shield rather than been placed upon it.

We also chose to tell the film without dialogue, allowing image, music and sound design to carry its narrative and emotional weight. This is not because these characters have no language, but because the story’s deepest emotions do not require translation. A hand reaching for another, a body bracing beneath a waterfall, the instinct to protect the urn before protecting oneself – these gestures tell us everything we need to know.

We wanted RORAIMA to communicate across language without becoming culturally anonymous – to feel rooted in a particular landscape while reaching for something instinctive in all of us.




PRODUCTION

Collaboration. With Ishan, Nick, Aishia and Kavin each living in a different country – and time zone – scheduling was essential to finishing the film on time. We treated RORAIMA like any other production from the outset: daily deliverables, regular status updates and weekly calls kept the work moving across continents.

Workflow. We always started with the image. We first created the film’s reusable visual foundations: character reference sheets, prop sheets and clean location plates. We first found art styles online from our favorite movies as inspiration. Then, iterating on YouTuber JOEY's freely-available Clade skills (thanks dude!), Claude helped us write and refine the specification for each asset, while GPT Image 2.0 inside Higgsfield generated the imagery. Once those images were locked, we animated them with Seedance 2.5 1080p, also inside Higgsfield. Claude helped us write the prompts to ensure consistent composition, performance, movement, camera, lighting, weather and sound. We also drew heavily from the prompt structure shared with Higgsfield’s animated short KÖK BÖRÜ. One of our favorite parts of working in this space is how openly filmmakers share their methods: each project leaves something behind that helps the next team express its ideas more precisely. We hope to do the same! ✌🏽🙂

All final image and video generation took place inside Higgsfield. The resulting material was then assembled through a conventional post-production workflow, where the edit, music, sound design and color were shaped together.

Roadblocks. Our most significant production hurdle came during a period of unusually high demand, when jobs submitted through Unlimited access repeatedly stalled and prevented us from generating the volume of material we needed. With a fixed schedule and limited budget, the interruption placed real pressure on the production. We contacted the Higgsfield support team, who reimbursed us and enabled us to redirect that budget into the credits needed to keep generating and complete the film. We were genuinely grateful for the resolution - a sincere shout-out to Christopher from the support team for helping us get the production moving again.

📝 Notes

Four practical lessons emerged from making RORAIMA.

AI performance must be directed from the outside in. Film school trains us to work with human actors from the inside out: discuss what a character wants or feels, then trust the performer to translate that inner state into behavior. AI reverses that process. “He is anguished” does not specify what physically changes within the frame. We had to describe the visible evidence - the lips parting, the eyes widening, the brows lifting, the breath changing.
More references do not necessarily create more control. In our experience, once we moved beyond roughly nine active reference images, elements began to mix and confuse one another. Although the model can technically accept more, a smaller and more carefully selected reference set produced more reliable results for us. Capacity and clarity are not always the same thing.
Know when to direct and when to discover. We found success at both ends of the spectrum. When we had a precise visual in mind, we specified the angle, framing and camera movement, and Seedance 2.5 often followed that direction remarkably well. At other times, we described the dramatic action and allowed the model to find the shots for us. The process worked best when we knew which decisions had to remain fixed and where surprise could be useful. For example, for many of the mountain climbing shots, the model was able to invent insane angles we never could have thought of.
“No background music” does not always mean no background music. One of our most persistent challenges was preventing Seedance 2.5 from generating its own score, even when the prompt explicitly requested no BGM. Because we wanted to construct the music and sound design independently in post-production, music baked into the generated clips made the edit considerably more difficult. We never found completely foolproof wording for this, and it remains one of the limitations we encountered.

Overall, making this film was a joy – but it was certainly a mountain of its own to scale. The journey tested us at every stage, yet reaching the summit made every difficult step worthwhile!
