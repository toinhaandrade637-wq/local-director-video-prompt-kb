Story

A tiny house mouse heads out from the safety of her attic in search of food. She knows exactly what she is looking for — but not where it will take her.

Following the smell of cheese, she makes her way through a world that was never built for something her size. What starts as a simple chase carries her further and further away from everything familiar.

The story is told entirely from her perspective: no dialogue, no explanations — just instinct, scent, sound, and the overwhelming feeling of being very small in a very big world.




World

The film uses a semi-photorealistic 3D style. It was important to me that the world feels believable, but not completely real — leaving just enough stylization to make the mouse feel like a character rather than a real animal dropped into a live-action scene.

Everything was built at true scale, from the mouse to the environments around her, so that the difference in size is felt in every shot. Fur, materials, lighting, physics and movement were all designed to give the world a convincing sense of weight and scale.




Colors

Color was an important part of the visual language throughout the film. The story gradually moves from warm orange and golden tones at the beginning to much colder colors towards the end.

The attic starts with a warm, almost cozy palette, while the world outside slowly loses that warmth. By the final part of the film, the image has shifted almost completely into cold blue and white tones.




Characters

The mouse is small, fast and constantly reacting to the world around her. She doesn't speak or behave like a human character — everything is driven by instinct, movement and the things she sees, hears and smells.

The humans are always experienced from her point of view. We never really see them as people — just legs passing overhead, hands appearing in front of her, footsteps, shadows and movement. Keeping them mostly out of frame makes the world feel much bigger without ever leaving her perspective.

Only at the very end do we finally see the person on the other side of her journey.




References & Visual Style

The character and fur work are closer to nature-documentary realism than classic character animation, but with a warmer and more expressive feel rather than something overly clinical.

For the camera, I looked more towards live-action adventure and chase cinematography: low, dynamic, sometimes handheld, and always close to mouse-eye level. I wanted the camera to feel like it was actually moving through the world with her, rather than floating around her like a typical 3D or video-game camera.




How It Was Made

The film was built shot by shot with Seedance, based on a detailed shot-by-shot script and locked character, prop and location reference sheets. These references helped keep the characters and environments consistent across dozens of individually generated clips.

The whole process was highly iterative. Every shot was tested, reviewed and re-directed, with many shots going through several generations before they felt right.

Editing and color grading were done in Premiere Pro, with masking, tracking and detail compositing in After Effects. Sound and music were also created using AI.




Why I made it

I don't get a lot of time to work on personal projects alongside my day job, so making a film like this meant squeezing it into whatever time I could find.

When I saw Edwin Catmull on the jury, though, I knew I wanted to give it a shot. I've read his book Creativity, Inc., and I've always been fascinated by the way he thinks about creativity, leadership and building an environment where people can do their best work. A lot of the principles he stands for have stuck with me, so the thought of him potentially seeing this film would honestly be an incredible honor.

And with Phedon Papamichael, Alex Proyas and Paul W. S. Anderson also on the jury, it's a pretty amazing group of people to have the chance to put this film in front of. Even if it simply makes it far enough for them to see it, I'd be incredibly happy.
