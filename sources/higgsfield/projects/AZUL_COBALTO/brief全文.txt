A magical-realism horror short set in Usera, Madrid, Spain. A story about fame, the body and ownership — deeply local, built for a global audience. Produced by Muscle Beach.

✨ Context

Azul Cobalto is my first narrative film fully directed and executed with AI.

My path started at seventeen on the animated series Pocoyo (Madrid), continued at nineteen at Buck (Los Angeles), at twenty-five at Golden Wolf (London), and at twenty-nine at CANADA (Barcelona), where I founded and led the directors' collective Manson. In late 2023 I released Romancero, a six-episode series I directed in its entirety for Prime Video, produced by The Mediapro Studio — and that was where I first started using AI instrumentally, generating visual concepts to communicate my ideas to the art and cinematography departments. In 2025 I founded Muscle Beach, a new media production company where we combine world-class craft with GenAI techniques, working at the front edge of advertising, music and now entertainment.

This short is the proof of that thesis: that you can tell a deeply local story with international ambition, and that a generative pipeline can carry a full narrative from beginning to end without ceasing to feel like cinema.

It also works in two directions. As a self-contained short film, and as a proof of concept and trailer for a long-form story — one that could be developed either full AI or live actions.

🎭 Narrative

Dani is twenty-one, with a cheap microphone and a gold grillz he never shows. Chen is the neighbour he has known all his life, a Chinese-Spanish ceramicist who can talk anyone into anything, and he decides to turn Dani into CH0KE. It works: within weeks Dani goes from invisible to impossible to ignore.




Then the pieces start going missing. A fan keeps a finger. A kid walks off with an ear. And Chen always has the replacement ready — white porcelain, hand-painted in cobalt blue, welded with gold. Everyone explains it the same way: fame makes you fragile.

Alba, Dani's girlfriend, watches all of it from the edge of the frame and understands none of it. Until she wakes one night to find a porcelain piercing on her bedside table that she never bought, and realises the porcelain was never a consequence of fame. It was Chen's choosing. And now it is her turn.

Theme. The film is about losing yourself when you put the focus outside. Dani doesn't have his identity taken from him — he stops inhabiting it, and porcelain fills the space he vacates. Everyone around him prefers the ceramic version. Chen isn't a thief; he's the man who spots the gap and fills it, beautifully, with something that shines better than the original. 

At the centre of the film is a line Dani raps at his first real gig: "It is almost impossible to be real in this fake world" It's the thesis, and it's doubled: a generated character in a generated film, saying out loud how hard it is to be real.

Structure. A bait-and-switch. The first half appears to be the story of a rapper; the second reveals that Alba is the subject and Chen was always the protagonist. The voiceover is Alba's statement in a police interview room, told from the end.

Audience. Genre and festivals. Adult, domestic, restrained horror — no blood, no jump scares. Closer to an unsettling fable than to a scare picture. It speaks to an audience that recognises Usera and to one that has never set foot in it.

🎨 Visuals

The canon. All porcelain is white with hand-painted cobalt-blue motifs. Gold exists only in the weld seams, on the exact line where ceramic meets skin: it is Chen's signature — a craftsman who doesn't honour the fracture but replaces the part. The only other gold in the film is Dani's grillz.

The rule of the eyes. The eyes are always real, wet and alive, until they aren't. They are the last measure of how much person is left inside.

References. The Usera exteriors are shot in the Safdie brothers' telephoto logic: compression, street level, distance. Interiors move between the vernacular — neighbourhood bar, slot machines, little Spanish flags — and the cold new money of the penthouse. The title sequence draws on Kyle Cooper and Fincher's Seven: clinical macro, surgical, illegible until the final frame.

Palette. Blue night and Chinese neon in the street. Domestic amber in Chen's flat. Flat, shadowless green fluorescent in the police station. And cobalt running through all of it.

Directorial gesture. Chen's look to camera is used exactly twice in the entire film, and the second one is the closing shot.

Poster. The key art follows the same way of thinking as the title sequence: one motif carrying the whole idea, nothing explained twice. A fragmented porcelain plate, cobalt-on-white, broken into pieces that still spell the title if read in order — the same object language as the film, working as a single still. The reference point is a mix between Kellerhouse Design and Prologue Studios' title-sequence work: the idea that an opening-credit sequence doesn't have to summarise a story so much as carry its soul, from a more graphic, more abstract place, before a single frame of story has run.

Four people, start to finish.

Pipeline. Mixed tools, primarily generative, assembled in traditional software with conventional production workflows. All generation was done in Higgsfield Cinema Studio 4: character, prop and location assets created as reusable elements, and the story built shot by shot from them. Same with the music and SFX, all generated in Suno and Elevenlabs.

Editing and sound design were resolved through a classic pipeline.

Method. The film was built on a closed system of character, prop and location references, generated once and reused in every shot to guarantee continuity. Each shot was written as a structured prompt with active references, spatial geography, physics, lighting and sound, and iterated against the result until validated.

Numbers. +300 clips generated. Final running time: 5:30 min. 21:9.

📣Reaching an audience

A film like this doesn't find its audience by sitting on a submissions page by itself, so we treated the launch as part of the craft, allocating it's own budget and timeline.

The first move was narrow and deliberate: a small push aimed at Almaty, where Higgsfield is headquartered, narrowing the targeted audience to those with interests in artificial intelligence, cinema, rap music, anime, etc... Not to move the vote — we were not sure if that audience mostly isn't the one voting — but to make sure the people running the festival saw the project in its first days, before the leaderboard did the deciding for us.

It worked. Higgsfield later granted the project production support to help finish the film — 20,000 credits, and about as clear a signal as it gets that the push had reached exactly who it needed to.

From there we widened out, following where the contest's own audience already lives rather than guessing. Reading the comment sections on the festival's leading entries showed English as the default language, a large and vocal Middle East/North Africa community, and almost no Spanish. We also looked at where Higgsfield's own user base was concentrated and ran short, few-day tests aimed at those territories to see which ones actually turned into interaction rather than just traffic. For a Madrid-set, Spanish-language story, that gap read as open water rather than a weakness — a natural home audience nobody else in the top ranks was talking to. So the push ran in parallel lanes — Spain and Latin America, the MENA community, the international English-speaking audience — each with its own creative, instead of one generic version everywhere.

Underneath that, the same rule applied organically: answer every comment, show up in the communities already talking about AI filmmaking, and let the film do the convincing once people clicked through.

There's a Hollywood instinct behind that discipline: a studio budgets marketing almost like a second production, spending nearly as much making sure a film is seen as it spent making it. We gave our own campaign the same weight — a good short film that nobody finds isn't finished, so reaching an audience mattered as much as the porcelain, the editing, or any other choice on screen.

None of that shows up in the shortlist math directly — views aren't part of that score, so sitting high on the most-watched list was never going to move it on its own. What it bought us was attention: the film sitting where Higgsfield's own users were already looking, long enough to be noticed and engaged with rather than just scrolled past. Followers, likes, and comments kept climbing through it, and that mattered to us more than the leaderboard position itself.

The push also reached further than the festival itself: producers we'd never pitched started getting in touch, and some of those conversations have already turned into opportunities we're working on now.

📝 Notes

Key decisions

The voiceover is Alba, giving a statement at a police station. Everything we see is retrospective, told by someone who understood it too late.
"Fame makes you fragile" is folklore, not a law of the world. It's what the neighbourhood tells itself so it doesn't have to look at Chen. Alba repeats it in the first half because back then she believed it too.
CH0KE is spelled with a zero. Always.
The gold belongs to Chen, not to the world. Only in the joins, never across the surface of the porcelain. That's the character's irony: he uses the technique of repair that honours the fracture to do exactly the opposite.
Chen looks at camera twice. He connects with the audience making them accomplish of his plan.
The horror is material, not supernatural. No doors opening by themselves, no ghosts: parts that unseat like a mannequin's, and people who notice nothing.
The officer doesn't believe her. The last link in a chain of people who look without seeing.

Process learnings

NSFW filters trigger on vocabulary, not content: "tear off", "undress", "getting dressed". Mannequin logic and detachable-component framing solve almost all of it.
Models fill in what's missing: to get a four-fingered hand you have to build it from scratch, digit by digit, rather than describe a hand with one missing.
Active references contaminate: a prop declared in the clip shows up where it shouldn't. The fix is to pull it out of the generation and shoot it separately.
Transitions hide the impossible: a state change inside a whip pan works; the same change on a slow move becomes a dissolve.
