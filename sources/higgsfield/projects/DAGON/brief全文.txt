Logline

A WWI-era draft evader, adrift alone at sea, washes up on a stretch of ocean floor risen impossibly above the waves — and finds something ancient waiting there. After rescue, he recounts it all to a quiet man in a locked room, unable to say whether any of it was real. In the present, ruined and haunted, he struggles with substance abuse as he plans to take his own life.

Format

A black-and-white short film. Narration-driven, dialogue-light, built as a looping psychiatric-interview framing device that is revealed partway through rather than established up front. Runtime target: short-form festival length.

Synopsis

England, 1915. As conscription tightens its grip, a man buys illegal passage to the United States aboard a merchant steamer. On the second night at sea the ship is set upon by a German raider. In the darkness and confusion he seizes a bag of provisions and the only lifeboat, and slips away as his crewmates are taken — a small, unforgivable act of self-preservation he spends the rest of his life justifying.

He drifts for uncounted days beneath a scorching sun. Then he wakes to find the sea gone: the ocean floor has risen beneath him, a vast black expanse of stinking, lifeless mire stretching to the horizon. Drawn toward a distant hill, he crosses the dead seabed and looks down into a moonlit chasm — where a single pale monolith stands, carved with inscriptions no human hand made. Something rises from the dark water behind it. What happens there marks him.

He is "rescued" — pulled from his lifeboat by a passing captain who takes him for a man deranged by thirst and solitude. But the vision never leaves him. The film's present tense reveals itself as an asylum interview: the man, recounting his story to a composed examiner. In the end, alone at a cold breakwater, he finishes what began on the risen seafloor — and the dark water answers.

Themes

Cowardice and the stories we tell to survive our own choices. The unreliability of memory and testimony. The insignificance of one frightened man against something older and vaster than mankind. The film withholds as much as it shows: the creature is never fully seen, the apocalypse never confirmed, the truth of the man's account never settled.

Visual & Tonal Approach
Black-and-white, with a soft, filmic tonal range — muted, gently rolled-off blacks that retain shadow detail rather than harsh, crushed contrast. A vintage, orthochromatic-leaning quality with period-honest optical character.
Documentary realism over polish. Uneven, single-source lighting; tactile material decay; naturalistic, slightly imperfect framing. The aim is a photographed, lived-in world rather than a rendered one.
Restraint as horror. The creature is shown only in fragments — a claw, a glimpsed limb, a shape in water. The dread is built through implication, stillness, and the man's reactions rather than direct display.
A consistent grammar across the film: deliberate framing, a period-accurate 1915 world, and a cold palette broken only where the story demands.
Source & Intellectual Property

The film is an original adaptation of the short story "Dagon" by H.P. Lovecraft, first published in 1917.

This source is in the public domain. Lovecraft died in 1937, and "Dagon" was published in 1917 — well before the 1929 cutoff after which works published in the United States entered the public domain. As a pre-1929 U.S. publication, the story carries no active copyright and is free to adapt, reproduce, and build upon without license or permission. It is widely reproduced in full by public archives on that basis.

All characters, incidents, and dialogue in the film are either drawn from this public-domain source or are original work created for this adaptation (including the framing device, the interview structure, the period-specific conscription material, and all invented dialogue). No protected third-party intellectual property is used or referenced in the film. The narration adapts and reworks passages from the public-domain text into an original script.
