Project brief

The body as stage, costume as ritual. A runway performance after Diaghilev’s Russian Seasons, the cinema of Almodóvar and Lynch, and ’90s couture.

I love the idea of a runway where each new character reveals more of the context of the show. These aren’t just beautiful looks. Each one sharpens the story rather than simply adding to it, and each one is meant to surprise, even to shock.

My main anchors were Diaghilev’s Russian Seasons and McQueen’s shows. The characters themselves are my personal memories of the actors in Almodóvar’s and Lynch’s films. Each of them holds me and my perception of those actors and the people they played. They’re my favourites, the ones who have been living in my head for years.ё

Mad Season is a runway performance, theatre and ballet at once. It plays like a memory that never happened, an unconscious déjà vu.




The Cane Woman and the Man in the Hat. After The Skin I Live In: a captive in a laboratory room and a surgeon watching her through a screen. It is the relationship between creator and creation, with gender erased.

Select generation




Agrado. After All About My Mother and High Heels. Two figures live in one body: Agrado, a trans character, and the woman she reassembles, a former singer and fading diva. Agrado wears this woman’s image and legend as her own. The body as a project.




The Saint. An Almodóvar heroine who is pure and holy, but also weak and torn, praying for salvation.




Lucía. The Saint grown up. She no longer cares about sin. She doesn’t pray; she talks on the phone and dictates the rules. She has stopped calling on power because she has become it.




The Femme Fatale. The finale is in Lynch’s universe. She carries Laura Palmer from Twin Peaks and Dorothy Vallens from Blue Velvet, a mystery and a wound at once.







The seizure. The central beat happens suddenly, on a strobe hit. The Man in the Hat catches the Cane Woman with one arm and drags her into the darkness at the far end of the runway.
Ruined beauty. Decay is more beautiful than bloom. The aesthetic draws on three houses at once:
From Galliano, the aftermath: the act has already happened, and what remains is melancholy and nostalgia.
From McQueen, the event: the act happens here and now, as a live performance.
From Gaultier, play with the body and gender: the corset, drag, costume as a second identity.

The face is constructed, not decorated.

The world. Everything happens in a dark industrial hangar with a metal-grated runway and a silent audience of silhouettes on both sides. The image is hard and clear, with high contrast and crisp detail, shot with the restless, close handheld camera of the Safdie Brothers. Every shot runs at 240fps under a hard strobe and strong wind. The finale breaks into a deep cobalt-blue monochrome.

Costume as second skin. A costume here isn’t clothing. It is the character’s shell. Texture and natural elements form its base: stone, feather, leather, and their prints. That is where a character shows best. Every costume was built to hold up in close-up, which works as a language of its own here.




What we learned:

Dance needs its own language. Precise ballet vocabulary (chassé, lunge, dip, extension) gave us actual choreography instead of generic motion.
Name the directors. Noé and the Safdie Brothers, and Lynch for the finale, conveyed the anxious character of the shots better than any adjectives.
Hard locks compete with each other. 240fps, strobe, wind, a limp and choreography in one shot is a lot of physics. The locks that matter most have to be stated as non-negotiable and repeated.
Balance the light. A location that is balanced in color gives cleaner, higher-quality generations, especially under a strobe.

Sound. The soundtrack was made by hand, inspired by Anetha, Blawan and EMF Reader.
